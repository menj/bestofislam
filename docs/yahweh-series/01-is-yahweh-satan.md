# Is Yahweh Satan? 7 troubling biblical parallels that raise serious questions

A question circulates in online debates and videos: is Yahweh, the God of the Hebrew Bible, really Satan? The case usually rests on a handful of passages in which Yahweh does something that a later text, or a modern reader, would expect of the devil. He incites a king to sin, sends a lying spirit, tests a righteous man to the edge of despair, and commands wars of destruction.

The passages are real, and they deserve a straight answer. This article sets out seven of them and asks what they show. Three explanations are on the table. The first is literal identity: Yahweh and Satan are one being. The second is functional parallel: some acts attributed to Yahweh resemble acts later attributed to Satan, without making the two the same. The third is historical development: the way the biblical writers spoke of divine and hostile power changed over the centuries, and the parallels mark the stages of that change.

One distinction runs through the whole series. Texts and archaeology can tell us how people spoke about Yahweh and about the adversary, when they did so and why. They cannot settle the identity of a supernatural being. A reader who wants to know what the evidence supports has to keep the two questions apart.

This is the hub of a five-part series, with parts on [the archaeology of Yahweh](https://bestofislam.org/who-was-yahweh-archaeological-origins/), [the divine council](https://bestofislam.org/was-yahweh-one-of-many-gods/), [Marcion and the Gnostics](https://bestofislam.org/early-christians-yahweh-evil-gnosticism-marcion/) and [the Islamic view](https://bestofislam.org/is-yahweh-the-same-as-allah/).

## 1. David's census: 2 Samuel 24:1 and 1 Chronicles 21:1

The parallel most often quoted comes from two tellings of one event. In the Book of Samuel, "Yahweh's anger burned against Israel, and he moved David against them, saying, 'Go, count Israel and Judah.'"<sup>[1]</sup> In the Book of Chronicles, the same census begins differently: "Satan stood up against Israel, and moved David to take a census of Israel."<sup>[2]</sup> One book names Yahweh as the instigator and the other names Satan.

For the identity hypothesis this is the decisive text: two authors describe one act and give it to two names, so the names must refer to one being. The argument has force only if both authors meant to describe the same agent in the same way, and the history of the books tells against that. Chronicles is a later work. Most scholars date it to the fourth century BCE, and its author used Samuel and Kings as sources, retelling them for a community after the exile.<sup>[3]</sup>

Samuel has Yahweh incite David and then punish Israel for the census, which raises an obvious moral problem. The Chronicler removes it by naming another agent. He did not think he was revealing the true name of Yahweh, since he goes on to describe Yahweh sending the plague, relenting, and accepting David's sacrifice in the same chapter.<sup>[4]</sup> An author who believed that Yahweh and Satan were one would have no reason to make the substitution at all.

In Hebrew, *satan* without the definite article can mean "an adversary", and some scholars, among them Peggy Day, read 1 Chronicles 21:1 as "an adversary stood up against Israel", with no developed figure of Satan in view.<sup>[5]</sup> Others read it as the first use of Satan as a proper name. Either way, the two verses show a later writer reassigning an act he found troubling. They do not show that he equated the two agents.

## 2. The lying spirit: 1 Kings 22:19–23 and Ezekiel 14:9

The prophet Micaiah describes a vision of Yahweh enthroned, with "all the army of heaven" standing beside him. Yahweh asks who will entice King Ahab to his death at Ramoth Gilead. A spirit volunteers: "I will go out and will be a lying spirit in the mouth of all his prophets." Yahweh answers, "You will entice him, and will also prevail. Go out and do so."<sup>[6]</sup> In Ezekiel, Yahweh says, "I, Yahweh, have deceived that prophet."<sup>[7]</sup> The New Testament later calls the devil "the father of lies" and the deceiver of the whole world.<sup>[8]</sup> Deception, later the work of Satan, is here commissioned by Yahweh.

The scene itself sets limits on the parallel. The lying spirit is one of the heavenly court, acting under command, and the deception is aimed at a king whom the narrative has already condemned. Most strikingly, the deception is disclosed. Micaiah tells Ahab to his face that a lying spirit has been sent and that Yahweh has spoken disaster against him.<sup>[6]</sup> Ahab hears the truth and chooses the lie. Ezekiel's verse belongs to the same pattern: it concerns a prophet who has already set up idols in his heart and goes on to prophesy to those who do the same.<sup>[7]</sup> The deception is presented as a judgement that confirms a choice already made.

Jewish interpreters were uneasy with the passage too. The Babylonian Talmud identifies the volunteering spirit with the spirit of Naboth, the man Ahab had killed, and has God send him out of the divine presence for offering to lie.<sup>[9]</sup> The tradition, like the Chronicler, moved the act away from God himself. The parallel is functional: the narrator presents deception as a punishment Yahweh permits, and never as his nature.

## 3. Testing and temptation: Genesis 22, Job 1–2 and James 1:13

"God tested Abraham," begins the story of the near-sacrifice of Isaac, and the test is to offer his son as a burnt offering.<sup>[10]</sup> In Job, the heavenly court gathers, the satan questions whether Job serves God for nothing, and Yahweh hands Job's family and property, and then his body, into the satan's power. Yahweh even says that the satan "incited me against him, to ruin him without cause".<sup>[11]</sup> The Letter of James, by contrast, says: "Let no man say when he is tempted, I am tempted of God: for God cannot be tempted with evil, neither tempteth he any man" (KJV).<sup>[12]</sup>

A test is meant to prove what is in a person; a temptation is an enticement to sin. Genesis frames the binding of Isaac as a test of faith, as the Letter to the Hebrews also reads it, while James speaks of the inner pull of desire, which he says belongs to the person himself.<sup>[13]</sup>

The later history of these stories is the stronger evidence. The Book of Jubilees, written in the second century BCE, retells Genesis 22 with a new opening: the prince Mastema comes before God and challenges Abraham's devotion, and the test follows.<sup>[14]</sup> Jubilees does the same with the strange night in Exodus when "Yahweh met Moses and wanted to kill him"; in its retelling the attacker is Mastema.<sup>[15]</sup> The pattern matches the Chronicler's. Later Jewish writers took acts they found hard to attribute to God and gave them to an adversary figure who acts within God's permission. That is development within a tradition, and it is the opposite of a claim that the two figures were always one.

## 4. War and destruction: Deuteronomy 20:16–18 and 1 Samuel 15:2–3

Deuteronomy commands Israel to "save alive nothing that breathes" in the cities of the land it is to occupy.<sup>[16]</sup> In 1 Samuel, Samuel tells Saul in the name of "Yahweh of Armies" to strike Amalek and kill "both man and woman, infant and nursing baby".<sup>[17]</sup> For many readers these are the hardest passages in the Bible, and the moral objection is serious. The identity argument adds a step: only a malevolent being could command this, so the being who commands it is Satan.

The objection is old, and believers have long answered it. Jewish tradition narrowed the commands. Maimonides ruled that even the war against the seven nations must begin with an offer of peace, and the Mishnah held that the ancient nations had been so mixed by Assyrian conquest that the old categories no longer applied.<sup>[18]</sup> Origen, the third-century Christian scholar, read the wars of Joshua as an allegory of the soul's battle with vice.<sup>[19]</sup> Modern scholars note that the language of total destruction was a convention of ancient Near Eastern war reports, and that the Bible itself records the same peoples surviving after the campaigns said to have destroyed them.<sup>[20]</sup> The Mesha Stele, a Moabite inscription of the ninth century BCE, uses the same word for devoting a captured town to its god, which places the practice in its regional setting; the inscription is discussed in [part 2 of this series](https://bestofislam.org/who-was-yahweh-archaeological-origins/).

None of these answers removes the difficulty for every reader, and the difficulty concerns the ethics of ancient warfare. In the Bible, Satan never commands Israel's wars. A reader may conclude that a text describes God wrongly, but the step from "this description is troubling" to "the being described is Satan" needs a further argument, and the passages do not supply it.

## 5. The ruler of this world: John 12:31, 2 Corinthians 4:4 and Luke 4:5–8

The New Testament gives Satan titles that sound like divine ones. In John, Jesus calls him "the prince of this world" and says he "will be cast out".<sup>[21]</sup> Paul writes of "the god of this world" who "has blinded the minds of the unbelieving".<sup>[22]</sup> In Luke, the devil shows Jesus all the kingdoms of the world and says their authority "has been delivered to me".<sup>[23]</sup> If the god who rules the world is Satan, the argument runs, and the God of the Hebrew Bible claims to rule the world, the two must be the same.

The passages themselves set Satan against the God of Israel. When the devil offers the kingdoms in exchange for worship, Jesus answers: "Get behind me Satan! For it is written, 'You shall worship the Lord your God, and you shall serve him only.'"<sup>[23]</sup> The quotation is from Deuteronomy, where "the Lord your God" stands for Yahweh.<sup>[24]</sup> Jesus refuses Satan by appealing to the worship of Yahweh. In John, the ruler of this world is about to be judged and driven out, which is the language of a usurper whose power is temporary. Mainstream Christian interpretation reads the titles this way: the rebellious world has a ruler, under the judgement of the Creator.<sup>[25]</sup>

The most quoted verse in this debate is John 8:44: "Ye are of your father the devil, and the lusts of your father ye will do" (KJV).<sup>[26]</sup> It has been used, with terrible consequences, against Jews as a people. Read in context, it is addressed to a particular group in a particular argument, introduced as "those Jews who had believed him", and it concerns their intention to kill him.<sup>[27]</sup> It is no statement about Jews collectively, and it is no statement about the God they worship. A few verses later Jesus says, "It is my Father who glorifies me, of whom you say that he is our God."<sup>[28]</sup> He identifies his Father with the God his hearers claim, which is the reverse of the identity hypothesis. The second-century thinkers who did separate the creator from the Father of Jesus are the subject of [part 4](https://bestofislam.org/early-christians-yahweh-evil-gnosticism-marcion/).

## 6. What the word *satan* meant, and how it changed

The Hebrew word *satan* means an adversary or accuser, and in most of its uses it names no supernatural being. The Philistine commanders fear that David will become "an adversary" to them in battle, and Yahweh raises up "an adversary to Solomon", Hadad the Edomite.<sup>[29]</sup> Most strikingly, when Balaam sets out to curse Israel, "Yahweh's angel placed himself in the way for an adversary against him", and the angel says, "I have come out as an adversary."<sup>[30]</sup> In that story, the satan is Yahweh's own messenger, sent to block a wrong path.

In Job and Zechariah the word takes the definite article: *ha-satan*, "the adversary" or "the accuser". The figure is a member of the heavenly court with a role to play, accusing and testing.<sup>[11]</sup> In Zechariah he accuses the high priest Joshua, and is rebuked: "Yahweh rebuke you, Satan!"<sup>[31]</sup> Yahweh and the accuser are two parties in one scene, and the first rebukes the second. When the Hebrew Bible was translated into Greek, the translators rendered the word as *diabolos*, the slanderer, the source of the English "devil".<sup>[32]</sup>

Over the following centuries the figure grew. The Enochic writings told of rebel angels; the Dead Sea Scrolls spoke of Belial and of a spirit of deceit, though the Community Rule says that God created both spirits; and by the time of the New Testament, Satan led a kingdom of evil powers.<sup>[33]</sup> Some scholars point to Persian influence, others to developments within Jewish thought such as the experience of persecution.<sup>[34]</sup> On the development itself there is wide agreement. The Satan of the New Testament is a later and fuller figure than the satan of Job, and the satan of Job is already distinct from Yahweh.

## 7. Identity, functional parallels or a developing theology?

Literal identity faces the texts in which the two appear together as distinct parties: the heavenly court of Job, the rebuke in Zechariah, the temptation in Luke where Jesus rejects Satan in the name of Yahweh, and the Chronicler's substitution, which only makes sense if he took them to be different. The hypothesis also proves too much. A God who tests the faithful and judges by permitting deception appears in other scriptures too, the Quran among them, as [part 5](https://bestofislam.org/is-yahweh-the-same-as-allah/) shows, so the argument would reach far beyond the Bible.

Functional parallels are real, and a reader is right to notice them.

Historical development explains the parallels best. Early Israelite writing attributes all causation to Yahweh. Isaiah has God say, "I form the light, and create darkness: I make peace, and create evil" (KJV), where the Hebrew word for evil covers calamity as well as wrongdoing.<sup>[35]</sup> Later writers placed the hostile and testing functions with subordinate agents, from the satan of the court to Mastema, Belial and the Satan of the New Testament. The parallels trace that process.

The evidence supports functional parallels and a developing theology. It does not establish that Yahweh and Satan are the same being, and several of the texts most often quoted for that claim state the opposite.

## The bottom line

The passages behind the question "is Yahweh Satan?" show a tradition wrestling with evil under one God, first by attributing everything to him and later by naming agents who act within his permission. The parts that follow take up the archaeology, the divine council, the early Christian dissenters and the Islamic account of God and Iblis. For a Muslim reader, the starting point is the Quran's own instruction to say to the People of the Book that "our God and your God is one", discussed in [7 reasons why Allah of the Quran is the same God as the God of the Bible](https://bestofislam.org/same-god/).<sup>[36]</sup>
