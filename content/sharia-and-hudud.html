Say "sharia" in a newsroom and the picture is a punishment. Say it in a mosque and the picture is a way of life: how to pray, how to trade, how to marry and how to be buried. Both are partly right, and mostly the first picture is wrong in proportion. Here are five common myths, and what the sources say.

The word sharī'a means a path to water. The Quran uses it once: "Then We put you on a clear path of the matter, so follow it."[1] It names God's guidance, and no book of statutes.

The detailed rulings are fiqh, the jurists' understanding of that guidance, and fiqh is human work. It is why the four Sunni schools, the Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali, differ on hundreds of points and are all accepted as valid. Ismail al-Faruqi's glossary of Islamic English draws the same line: sharī'a is God's legislation, the laws of Islam as given, and fiqh is knowledge of Islam through its laws, the human science of deriving them.[2]

The ḥudūd ("limits") are the fixed punishments for a handful of offences: theft, highway robbery, adultery, false accusation of adultery, and for some jurists drinking and apostasy. A typical book of law runs to hundreds of pages on worship, trade, contracts, marriage, inheritance, endowments and courtesy, and gives a few pages to the hudud.

A way to see the proportion is to read the Quran itself. The verses that set punishments are a small fraction of the whole, and the overwhelming weight is on belief, character and obligations to others.

The opposite is true. The jurists made the hudud almost impossible to prove. Adultery needs four eyewitnesses to the act itself, and a person who accuses without them is flogged for slander.[3] The Prophet is reported to have said: "Avert the ḥudūd from the Muslims as far as you can."[4] When a man named Ma'iz confessed to adultery, the Prophet turned away from him several times and asked if he had perhaps only kissed or touched, until Ma'iz insisted.[5]

Doubt cancels the penalty. Umar suspended the penalty for theft during the year of famine, because people were stealing from need.[6] Jurists drew from such cases that a hadd (the singular of hudud) may be carried out only when intent, proof and the absence of any excuse are all clear.

The Quran attaches repentance to almost every hadd. "Those who repent after that and make amends, God is forgiving and merciful."[7] Highway robbers who repent before they are caught are excused.[8]

For killing, the law allows the family of the victim to forgive or accept blood money, and the Quran calls forgiveness better: "Whoever forgives and makes reconciliation, his reward is with God."[9] The system is built to give the family the choice to stop at mercy.

Islamic law has an internal tool for new questions, ijtihād, the scholar's disciplined effort to reach a ruling where the text is silent. The Prophet said that a judge who exercises ijtihad and is right has two rewards, and if he errs has one.[10]

The old claim that "the gate of ijtihad was closed" in the tenth century is rejected by many historians, who show that jurists went on issuing new opinions for centuries, and by modern scholars who ask that it be used more freely. Most of the rulings on banking, medicine and technology today come from just this process.
