Critics sometimes say that no ancient source outside Islam mentions Mecca or its sanctuary. Defenders sometimes answer with every ancient text that might. Neither serves the reader.

Here are five witnesses that are often cited for the Kaaba (Kaʿbah, "the cube") before Islam, each weighed for what it can bear.

In the first century BC the Greek historian Diodorus of Sicily, describing the Arabian coast of the Red Sea, wrote of a temple there "very holy and exceedingly revered by all Arabians".[1]

In the eighteenth century Edward Gibbon took this for the Kaaba. He also raised the difficulty himself: Agatharchides, the earlier writer whom Diodorus follows for the rest of the passage, does not mention the temple.[2] Diodorus places it on a coastal gulf among a people he calls the Banizomenes, while Mecca lies inland.[1] The identification remains possible and unproven.

In the second century AD the geographer Ptolemy listed a town in western Arabia called Macoraba. Scholars have suggested since 1646 that it is Mecca.[3]

The historian G. W. Bowersock accepts the identification, reading the name as Makkah with an Aramaic adjective meaning "great".[4] Patricia Crone rejects it outright.[5] Ian Morris, in a sixty-page study in 2018, agrees with Crone that Ptolemy's coordinates are too inexact to settle the question on their own.[6] This witness is contested, and honest use of it says so.

In 2025 a study in a Cambridge journal published a newly found inscription in Qatabanic, an ancient language of South Arabia, that uses the word kʿbt, the first time the term has been found in that language.[7]

The study proposes a South Arabian origin for the name of the pre-Islamic sanctuary at Mecca, which Arabic tradition derives from kaʿb, "cube".[7] It bears on the history of the name, which it places among the religious vocabulary of ancient Arabia; the sanctuary at Mecca itself lies outside its scope.

A psalm describes pilgrims who pass through the valley of Baca, as many translations render it, and make it a place of springs.[8] Because the Quran calls Mecca by an older name, Bakkah (Bakkah, the Quran's name for Mecca), some have read the psalm as a reference to it.

The Jewish Encyclopedia notes that the name signifies a valley lacking water, and records the view that it comes from an Arabic word meaning "lacking in streams".[9] But the psalm's pilgrims are travelling to Zion, in Jerusalem.[10] A standard biblical reference work observes that a valley near Mecca lies wholly outside the region the passage requires.[11] The psalm shares a word with the Quran's name for Mecca, and nothing more; offered as evidence for Mecca, it would mislead.

The Quran makes the claim directly: "the first House [of worship] established for mankind was that at Makkah", where the Arabic uses the older name Bakkah. It is blessed and a guidance, with "the standing place of Abraham", and a pilgrimage to it is owed to God by all who are able.[12]

That claim rests on the Quran's authority, which the site defends on its own terms elsewhere. It does not need a Greek historian or a Roman geographer to carry it.

The bottom line

Of five witnesses, one is the Quran itself. Two are classical texts that may refer to the Kaaba and remain disputed; one inscription bears on the history of its name; and one psalm, often cited, bears on it not at all.

Arguing well means saying which is which. A case built on the disputed texts would fall with them; a case built on the Quran, with the classical evidence weighed openly beside it, stands.
