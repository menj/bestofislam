Muslims have read European and American scholars of Islam for a long time, with a mix of use and objection. Here are five who shaped the field, what each argued, and where Muslim scholars have answered.

Ismail al-Faruqi, a Muslim scholar who taught in North America, is a good guide to the objection. He complained that orientalist art historians called the arabesque mere "decoration", and never asked why Islam turned away from figures, which he argued is a deliberate expression of the oneness of God.[1] His complaint is about categories that fit one culture's frame but not another's. The same worry runs through the five below.

Geiger, a German rabbi and scholar, wrote a prize essay in 1833, Was hat Mohammed aus dem Judenthume aufgenommen? ("What did Muhammad take over from Judaism?"). He argued that the Quran borrows from Jewish sources.

Muslim scholars answer that the Quran says it confirms earlier scripture (5:48). Similarity is what its own claim predicts. They also note that Geiger's argument starts from the assumption it sets out to prove.

Caetani, an Italian prince and historian, wrote Annali dell'Islam, a ten-volume chronological history of early Islam, built on the Arabic sources. He was sceptical about many of the early reports.

The value of his work is the collection of material. Muslim scholars reply that the science of hadith criticism, with its study of chains and narrators, answers the problem of reliability, and that Caetani did not engage it.

Jeffery, an Australian professor in Cairo and New York, produced Materials for the History of the Text of the Qur'an (1937), a collection of variant readings, and The Foreign Vocabulary of the Qur'an (1938), listing words of non-Arabic origin.

Muslim scholars answer that variant readings (qirāʾāt) are known and traced to the Prophet, and that Arabic, like any language, has loanwords. They say the Quran's claim to be in "clear Arabic" refers to its clarity.[2]

Goitein, a German-born scholar of the Jewish tradition, spent his career on the Cairo Geniza, the huge cache of medieval documents, and wrote A Mediterranean Society. His work shows ordinary Jewish and Muslim life in the medieval world: trade, marriage, letters, quarrels.

Muslim historians cite his picture of Jews and Muslims living and trading together as evidence of the tolerance of the medieval Islamic world.

Armstrong, a British writer and former nun, wrote Muhammad: A Biography of the Prophet (1991) and later works. She presents the Prophet as a man of religious and social purpose, and Islam as a religion of mercy. She has criticised hostility to Islam in the West.

Her admirers are many among Muslims. Some Muslim scholars respond that a sympathetic outsider still cannot say what it means to be a believer.
