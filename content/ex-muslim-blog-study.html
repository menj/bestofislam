In 2019 two researchers at Universiti Sultan Zainal Abidin in Terengganu published a close study of a blog kept by a Malaysian woman who had left Islam.[1] They analysed how she presented herself, how she told her story, how she persuaded her readers, and how those readers answered her.

The subject matters in Malaysia more than in most places. The constitution defines a Malay as a person who professes Islam, so a Malay who leaves the faith also loses a legal identity, with material consequences that can include a divided family.[2] The blogger is left unnamed here. The study names her, with her consent, and is cited in full below.

What follows is what the researchers found, and what it means for Muslims who answer such writing.

The study found that the blogger built two identities through her writing: an opponent of religion, and an ordinary, conversational blogger.[3] She wrote as someone intelligent and unafraid, and she invited readers to question her decision to leave.

That invitation did work of its own. By opening a space for argument, the researchers observe, she reduced her critics to those willing to argue with her on intellectual terms.[3]

Almost every entry was a story from her own life, above all the turning point at which she left Islam.[4] The researchers note that storytelling lets a writer place readers inside her experience, so that they feel what she felt before they weigh what she claims.

A story is hard to rebut with a proof text. A reader who has just shared someone's distress is poorly placed to hear a verse quoted against her.

The researchers found all three classical modes of persuasion in the blog: appeals to her credibility, to readers' emotions and to reasoning.[5] Her appeals to emotion rested heavily on the insults and threats she had received, told so that readers would sympathise with her and support her.

They add a caution of their own: sympathy of this kind can cloud a reader's judgement about what is true.[5] That caution applies to any testimony, from any direction, and it is worth keeping in mind before either accepting or dismissing one.

The researchers classified the comments readers left. Of those analysed, 174 were supportive, about 40 percent; 109 were hostile, about 25 percent; and 36 offered advice, about 8 percent.[6]

The supporters were moved chiefly by sympathy for what she described. Many added experiences of their own, which kept the conversation going and bound her readers more closely to her.[6]

The hostile comments fell into two kinds. Some were insults with no argument, written to vent anger with no expectation of an answer. Others questioned her beliefs with statements and evidence, and invited a reasoned reply.[7] Insults and threats, as the third finding above describes, were at the heart of the sympathy she drew from readers.

The Muslim readers who offered advice did something more skilful. They were firm in asking her to stop attacking Islam and gentle in inviting her back, and they combined reasons with an appeal to her feelings. The researchers judge that with sound reasons and the right approach, such replies might have changed her mind.[7]

The bottom line

The study reads as a warning addressed to Muslims as much as a portrait of one blogger. Her opponents' abuse became part of her case, since accounts of insults and threats drew readers' sympathy. The replies the researchers thought could reach her were the ones that answered her reasons and treated her with respect.

That is the standard this site tries to keep. Answer the argument, and leave the person her dignity.
