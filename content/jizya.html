Few words from Islamic history are used against Islam as often as jizya (jizyah, the poll tax on non-Muslim subjects). Critics call it a penalty for unbelief; defenders call it a fee for protection and exemption from military service.

The sources support parts of both accounts. Here are six facts, with the critics' strongest points stated plainly.

An early and influential manual of Islamic taxation was written around 790 by Abū Yūsuf, chief judge to the caliph Hārūn al-Rashīd. It lays the jizya on the men among the non-Muslim subjects, sparing women and children, and sets it in three annual rates: forty-eight dirhams for the wealthy, twenty-four for those of middling means, and twelve for the needy, the labourer and the workman.[1]

A graduated rate by ability to pay was typical of the tax as the jurists described it, though practice varied across centuries and regions.

The obligation rests on one verse, which commands Muslims to fight certain of the People of the Book "until they give the jizyah willingly while they are humbled".[2] The final word, ṣāghirūn, is the centre of the dispute: other translations render it "being brought low".[2]

Classical scholars read it in opposite ways. The commentator al-Zamakhshari described a collection meant to humiliate the payer, while Abū Yūsuf, in the same tax manual, ruled that no payer should be beaten or mistreated to extract it, and that collectors should be lenient.[3] The verse itself settles the obligation; how the payer was to be treated was argued among Muslims from the start.

Women, children, monks devoted to worship, and those unable to work were exempt across the major schools of law.[1][4]

Critics are right, however, that poverty alone did not exempt a man. The Hanafi and Hanbali manuals held able-bodied poor men who could earn a living, such as labourers and artisans, liable for the lowest rate; exemption was reserved for those unable to work at all.[4]

Abū Yūsuf records that when the Muslim commander Abū ʿUbaydah could no longer guarantee the protection of towns in Syria, he returned the jizya he had collected from them.[4] Even critics of the tax acknowledge the account.[4]

The episode shows that at least some early Muslim authorities understood the tax as one side of a bargain, with the state's protection as the other.

Later practice made the connection explicit. The Jewish Encyclopedia of 1906 describes the Ottoman poll tax as the one "which took the place of military service".[5] In the reforms of 1856 the Ottoman state replaced it with a tax for exemption from military service, the bedel-i askerî.[6]

Critics reply that conquerors would never have wanted conquered unbelievers in their armies, so the exemption explains little.[7] Both points can stand: the tax marked a subject who did not bear arms for the state.

The tax fell on non-Muslims because they were non-Muslims, and a man who became Muslim ceased to owe it.[4] It was therefore a mark of religious status as well as a charge for services, and an honest defence should say so.

Muslims, for their part, owed the zakat (zakāh, the obligatory alms), a religious levy of their own; some scholars see the two as parallel obligations falling on the two communities.[8]

The bottom line

The jizya was a graduated annual tax on adult non-Muslim men, with real exemptions and real limits to them. It was linked to protection and to service in arms, and it marked a difference of religion; Muslims argued among themselves over how its payers should be treated.

In the Ottoman lands it ended with the reforms of 1856. A fair account holds all of this together, which is more persuasive than either the critic's caricature or the defender's simplification.
