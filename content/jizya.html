Few words from Islamic history are used against Islam as often as jizya (jizyah, the poll tax on non-Muslim subjects). Critics call it a penalty for unbelief; defenders call it a fee for protection and exemption from military service. Both descriptions circulate widely online, and each usually leaves out the half that does not suit it.

The sources support parts of both accounts. Here are six facts, drawn from the Quran, the early legal manuals and later history, with the critics' strongest points stated plainly and answered where the evidence allows.

An early and influential manual of Islamic taxation was written around 790 by Abū Yūsuf, chief judge to the caliph Hārūn al-Rashīd. It lays the jizya on the men among the non-Muslim subjects, sparing women and children, and sets it in three annual rates: forty-eight dirhams for the wealthy, twenty-four for those of middling means, and twelve for the needy, the labourer and the workman.[1]

The Hanafi jurists, whose school Abū Yūsuf helped to found, kept this sliding scale. Other schools followed the report that the Prophet (peace be upon him), when he sent Muʿādh ibn Jabal to Yemen, told him to take one dinar from each adult.[2] The Shāfiʿī school generally treated that dinar as the standard sum, so the jurists were not agreed on the figure.

A graduated rate by ability to pay was typical of the tax as the jurists described it, though practice varied across centuries and regions.

The obligation rests on one verse, revealed in the ninth year after the migration to Medina, when the Muslim community was facing the Byzantine frontier. It commands Muslims to fight those among the People of the Book who do not believe in God and the Last Day, "until they give the jizyah willingly while they are humbled".[3] The final word, ṣāghirūn, is the centre of the dispute: other translations render it "being brought low".[3]

Classical scholars read it in opposite ways. The commentator al-Zamakhshari described a collection meant to humiliate the payer, while Abū Yūsuf, in the same tax manual, ruled that no payer should be beaten or mistreated to extract it, and that collectors should be lenient.[4] Al-Shāfiʿī is generally reported to have read the word as submission to the authority of Muslim law.[5] The verse itself settles the obligation; how the payer was to be treated was argued among Muslims from the start.

The jurists also differed over who could pay. The Shāfiʿī school limited the choice to the People of the Book and the Zoroastrians, while the Mālikī school accepted it from a wider range of non-Muslims.

Women, children, monks devoted to worship, and those unable to work were exempt across the major schools of law.[1][6] Abū Yūsuf also records a letter of the commander Khālid ibn al-Walīd to the people of al-Ḥīra in Iraq, which promised that an old man unable to work, or one struck by calamity, would be freed from the tax and maintained from the public treasury.[1]

Critics are right, however, that poverty alone did not exempt a man. The Hanafi and Hanbali manuals held able-bodied poor men who could earn a living, such as labourers and artisans, liable for the lowest rate; exemption was reserved for those unable to work at all.[6]

The same manual tells of the caliph ʿUmar seeing an aged non-Muslim begging at a door. He said that the community had not done justice to the man if it took the tax from him in his youth and left him to beg in old age, and he ordered him supported from the treasury.[1] The story is about a recognised rule, and also about the hardship the tax could cause where the rule was not applied.

Abū Yūsuf records that when the Muslim commander Abū ʿUbaydah could no longer guarantee the protection of towns in Syria, he returned the jizya he had collected from them.[6] The geographer al-Balādhurī reports the same event at Homs, in the year of the battle of Yarmuk in 636, when a Byzantine army was gathering against the Muslim forces.[7] Even critics of the tax acknowledge the account.[6]

The episode shows that at least some early Muslim authorities understood the tax as one side of a bargain, with the state's protection as the other. Abū ʿUbaydah told the townspeople that the Muslims could no longer defend them, so the payment had to go back.

A critic may reply that one story from a time of war is a thin basis for a general principle. The reply has force, and the jurists did not rest the principle on the story alone: they described the payer as a person under a covenant of protection, and the loss of protection as a breach on the ruler's side.

Later practice made the connection explicit. The Jewish Encyclopedia of 1906 describes the Ottoman poll tax as the one "which took the place of military service".[8] In the reforms of 1856 the Ottoman state abolished the jizya and replaced it with a tax for exemption from military service, the bedel-i askerî, which non-Muslim men paid in place of serving.[9]

Critics reply that conquerors would never have wanted conquered unbelievers in their armies, so the exemption explains little.[10] Both points can stand: the tax marked a subject who did not bear arms for the state.

The link between the two was real in the other direction as well. Some early surrender treaties are reported to have excused a community from the tax in return for serving alongside the Muslim army. That arrangement treats the payment and the service as alternatives, though the surviving reports of it are few and come mostly from historians writing generations later.

Ismail al Faruqi, describing the Islamic state as he understood it, states the link directly. The duty of defensive war falls on Muslims alone; non-Muslim citizens may volunteer, and those who do are exempt from the jizya and treated exactly as Muslims, but the state may not conscript them.[11]

The tax fell on non-Muslims because they were non-Muslims, and a man who became Muslim ceased to owe it.[6] It was therefore a mark of religious status as well as a charge for services, and an honest defence should say so.

Muslims, for their part, owed the zakat (zakāh, the obligatory alms), a religious levy of their own; some scholars see the two as parallel obligations falling on the two communities.[12] Zakat took a fixed share of wealth above a minimum, whereas jizya was assessed on the adult man.

The strongest form of the objection is financial pressure to convert. Historians of the early Umayyad period record that governors sometimes kept charging the tax to new converts because the revenue was needed, and that the caliph ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz ended the practice early in the eighth century.[13] The answer is that the Quran declares there is no compulsion in religion (2:256), and that Christian, Jewish and other communities lived under Muslim rule for centuries, which would not have happened if the tax had worked as a device for conversion.

The bottom line

The jizya was a graduated annual tax on adult non-Muslim men, with real exemptions and real limits to them. It was linked to protection and to service in arms, and it marked a difference of religion; Muslims argued among themselves over how its payers should be treated.

Neither slogan survives the sources. A pure fee for services would not have fallen on people for their religion, and the Quran speaks of the payers as humbled. A pure penalty would not have come with manuals that protect the payer from abuse, exempt the helpless and tie the payment to a promise of defence. In the Ottoman lands it ended with the reforms of 1856. A fair account holds all of this together, which is more persuasive than either the critic's caricature or the defender's simplification.
