Two arguments about the text of the Quran circulate side by side. The first holds that the Prophet allowed seven ways of reciting, so that the printed Quran of today is only one of them. The second holds that verses went missing, with the stoning verse and a sheep as the best-known example.

The four points below take the reports on the seven modes, the difference between the modes and the readings, the 1924 Cairo edition and the stoning verse in turn.

Umar ibn al-Khattab once heard Hisham ibn Hakim reciting Surat al-Furqan in a way Umar had not been taught, and brought him to the Prophet. The Prophet approved both recitations as revealed and said that the Quran was revealed in seven ways (ahruf, singular harf), so that people should recite what comes easiest to them.[1]

The report is the foundation of the later study of the readings. It places variation inside the Prophet's own lifetime and under his approval, which answers the charge that the variants arose from later confusion.

Critics often equate the seven ahruf with the seven canonical readings (qira'at) and then ask why the printed text follows only one. SeekersGuidance, summarising the classical literature, states that the two are different. The ahruf were seven valid modes of recitation present in the Prophet's lifetime, while the readings are methods attributed to named reciters and transmitted through chains, and they were fixed as a recognised set in the fourth century of the Hijra.[2]

Ibn Mujahid (d. 324 AH) singled out seven well-known readings in his book on the subject, and Ibn al-Jazari later added three to make ten. Scholars disagreed on how much of the seven ahruf the Uthmanic codices preserved: al-Baqillani held that the codices together contained all of them, while another view holds that only part remains, since Uthman had the scribes follow the dialect of Quraysh when they differed.[2] Al-Qurtubi, in the introduction to his commentary, records the position of many Maliki scholars, among them al-Dawudi and Ibn Abi Safra, that the seven famous readings are not the seven ahruf in which the Companions were permitted to recite. On that view the readings go back to one of the seven, the one on which Uthman united the codex, and the famous readings are the choices of the imams, each of whom selected what he judged best from what he had received. None of them rejected the choice of another.[3] The classical sources record the disagreement openly.

The edition printed in Cairo under King Fu'ad and associated with al-Azhar is often described as the moment the Quran was standardised. A conference of the French Institute of Oriental Archaeology in Cairo records that the edition was dated 1919 in its colophon, though it is customarily dated 1924, a date linked with the abolition of the caliphate, and that al-Azhar saw it as a way of filling the gap that abolition left.[4]

The same account notes that the edition carried appendices on abrogation, revelation and the seven readings, and that it became the main reference for European researchers after 1950 until the Medina edition of 1985 replaced it.[4] The edition is therefore a printing project with a political setting, and the sources in this section do not describe it as a change to the text.

Critics cite Umar's statement that the verse of stoning was among the things God revealed, that the Prophet stoned and the Muslims did so after him, and that Umar feared people would say that they could not find the verse in the Book of God.[5] They pair it with a report of A'isha, in Ibn Majah, that the verse of stoning and of suckling an adult ten times was written on a paper under her pillow, and that a domestic sheep ate it when the Prophet died.[6]

The scholars who examined the first report treated it under the heading of abrogation of wording while the ruling stays (naskh al-tilawa duna al-hukm). Abu Amina Elias, citing al-Bayhaqi, states that the ruling is established and the recitation abrogated, with no known disagreement, and that stoning for the married adulterer was carried out under the Prophet, Abu Bakr and Umar.[7] The ruling therefore rests on the practice of the Prophet and his successors, and does not depend on the wording being printed in the codex. The second report carries a grading of hasan in the Darussalam edition, and the Quran's text rests on the mass transmission of the community, not on a single sheet of paper in one house.[6] The chain and the gradings of that report receive their own treatment in the article on the sheep and the sheet.
