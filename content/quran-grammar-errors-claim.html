A recurring argument against the Quran holds that it contains grammatical mistakes, and that a book with mistakes cannot meet its own challenge to produce anything like it. The argument rests on a handful of verses and on one report attributed to A'isha.

The four points below take the challenge first and then the verses the critics cite, with what the classical commentators said about each.

The Quran tells those in doubt about it to bring a single chapter (sura) like it, and to call on their helpers other than God, if they are truthful.[1] Another verse states that if humans and jinn joined forces to produce something like the Quran, they would fail, even if they backed one another.[2]

Critics answer that a text full of grammatical faults cannot be a model of eloquence, and they name three verses. The challenge stands or falls on those examples, so each is taken below as the critics present it.

In 4:162 a list of believers runs in the nominative case, then one phrase, wa al-muqimina al-salat (and those who establish prayer), appears in the accusative, before the list returns to the nominative. Al-Tabari recorded the report that A'isha, asked about this verse, about 5:69 and about 20:63, said that it was the work of the scribes, who made errors in writing.[3]

Al-Tabari rejected the scribal-error explanation. He noted that the codex of Ubayy ibn Ka'b read the same way, and argued that if a scribe had erred, the other codices would differ from ours. He added that the Companions who taught the Quran would have corrected any fault in recitation, and that the whole community transmitted the reading as written.[4]

He also recorded the explanation of grammarians of Kufa and Basra: Arabs shift the case of a descriptive phrase to single it out for praise (nasb ala al-madh, the accusative of praise) when a description grows long, citing 2:177, where al-sabirina (the patient) stands in the accusative within a nominative list.[3] Al-Tabari preferred a third reading, in which the phrase is genitive and refers to the angels, but none of the options he weighed requires an error.[4]

Critics hold that after inna the demonstrative should be hadhayni, in the accusative, and that hadhani is wrong. The Hafs reading, printed in the standard Medina text, reads in hadhani la-sahirani (these two are surely sorcerers), with the lightened particle in, after which the nominative is regular.[5]

For the readers who pronounced inna, al-Tabari explained the form as the usage of the Banu al-Harith ibn Ka'b, Khath'am, Zubayd and neighbouring Yemeni tribes, who keep the dual in -ani in every case. He cited verse in that dialect and quoted Abu Ubayda's report that he had heard tribes of Kinana do the same.[6]

The deeper problem with the objection is one of sequence. The rules against which critics measure these verses were compiled by grammarians who took the Quran and pre-Islamic poetry as their evidence. Al-Tabari's discussion of 4:162 and 20:63 shows the method: when a construction seemed irregular, scholars looked for the same usage in the speech of the Arabs, and found it.[4][6]
