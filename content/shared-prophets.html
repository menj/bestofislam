The Quran assumes you know these men already. It tells their stories in fragments, as reminders, the way you would mention a story everyone in the room has heard. So the interesting question is never whether the Quran has the same prophets. It is what it changes.

Both: created by God, placed in a garden, disobeyed, expelled.

The difference is in what follows. In Genesis the disobedience has consequences that run down every generation. In the Quran, Adam repents, God forgives him, and the matter is closed.[1] There is no original sin. Every child is born clean. That single divergence sits under the whole later dispute about why Jesus came.

Both: a flood, an ark, a family saved.

The Quran adds a son who refuses to board, climbs a mountain instead, and drowns.[2] The point is made explicitly: kinship saves nobody. Noah's own child is lost because he did not believe. It is a harder story than the Genesis one, and the Quran tells it to remove any idea that faith is inherited.

Both: the father of the faith, tested by a command to sacrifice his son.

The Bible names the son as Isaac. The Quran does not name him, and the majority Muslim view is that it was Ishmael.[3] Beyond that, the Quran gives Abraham a story the Bible does not: as a young man he breaks his father's idols and argues with his people about worshipping things they made themselves. In the Quran he is a reasoner before he is a patriarch.

The closest match of the 6. The Quran tells the whole story in one chapter, and it follows Genesis closely: the dream, the brothers, the well, Egypt, the prison, the interpretation, the famine, the reunion.

What differs is the woman. In Genesis, Potiphar's wife is a villain and vanishes. In the Quran she has a name in later tradition, Zulaykhā, and a scene in which she confesses publicly that Joseph was innocent and she was at fault.[4] The story lets her repent.

Both: the basket, Pharaoh, the burning bush, the plagues, the sea, the tablets, the golden calf.

The Quran mentions Moses more than any other prophet, and the emphasis falls on his confrontation with Pharaoh as a confrontation between a messenger and a tyrant who claims divinity. Aaron is a co-prophet, sent alongside him at Moses's own request.[5] And the Quran has Moses meet an unnamed teacher who shows him that things which look unjust have reasons he cannot see.[6] That episode has no biblical parallel at all.

Both: virgin birth, miracles, disciples, Messiah.

The Quran adds miracles the gospels do not have: Jesus speaking as an infant in the cradle, and forming a bird from clay and breathing life into it.[7] It removes the crucifixion as an accomplished death, saying it was made to appear so.[8] And it removes divinity. He is the Messiah, a word from God, and a servant. The reasons Muslims hold that position are set out in a separate piece.

The bottom line

Same men, and in most cases the same events. The changes cluster around 3 things: no inherited sin, no salvation by kinship, no divine sonship.

Read the Quran's versions as corrections and the pattern is obvious. Each one removes an idea that later theology built on. Whether that is restoration or revision is the argument between the 2 faiths, and it is an argument about these 6 stories before it is about anything else.
