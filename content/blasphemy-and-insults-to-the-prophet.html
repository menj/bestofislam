Each new controversy over a cartoon, a film or a burned Quran brings the same charge: Islam punishes criticism with death, and Muslims riot when their Prophet is mocked. The charge mixes scripture, classical law, modern statute and street violence into one picture.

The four points below separate them: what the Quran says about mockery, how the Prophet answered insults, what the classical jurists held, and where the best-known modern blasphemy law comes from.

The Quran expected Muslims to hear insults. It tells them that they will hear much that hurts from those given scripture before them and from the polytheists, and that patience and mindfulness of God are a mark of resolve.[1] When people mock God's verses, believers are told to leave the gathering until the talk turns to something else.[2]

The Prophet himself was told to proclaim his message and turn away from the polytheists, with the assurance that God would suffice him against the mockers.[3] The same scripture forbids Muslims to insult the gods others worship, lest they insult God in ignorance.[4] The instruction is restraint in both directions.

A group of Jews greeted the Prophet with al-samu alaykum, "death be upon you." A'isha answered them in kind. The Prophet told her to be calm, since God loves gentleness in every matter, and replied to the men only with "and upon you."[5]

A'isha reported that after the Prophet was rejected by Ibn Abd Yalil and walked away in distress, the angel of the mountains offered to crush the people between two mountains. The Prophet declined, saying that he hoped God would bring from their descendants people who would worship God alone.[6]

The classical schools did treat insulting the Prophet as a grave offence, and critics are right that some jurists prescribed death for it; Mazhar notes a Hanafi report that a Muslim blasphemer is killed without the chance to repent, which he argues is a misreading.[7] The schools differed. Arafat Mazhar has set out the Hanafi position from its classical texts: Abu Hanifa held that a non-Muslim who insults the Prophet is not killed for it, since non-Muslims are not killed for unbelief, which is the greater wrong; al-Quduri held that such an insult does not break the non-Muslim's covenant of protection; and al-Tahawi prescribed a warning for a first offence and a lesser penalty for repetition.[7]

In the rulings Mazhar surveys, any penalty beyond a warning lies with the ruler and the court, and it is applied by the state.[7]

The country most cited in this debate is Pakistan. Its blasphemy provisions are rooted in the Indian Penal Code of 1860, drafted under British rule; section 295-A was added by the British in 1927 to restrain tension between Hindus and Muslims, and passed into Pakistani law at partition.[8] Section 295-C, which made derogatory remarks about the Prophet punishable by death or life imprisonment, was added in 1986.[8]

The modern statute is therefore a colonial framework extended by a twentieth-century legislature. Its mandatory sentences, which allow no warning or repentance, depart from the Hanafi rulings set out above.[7]
