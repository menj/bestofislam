Extremist groups today call other Muslims unbelievers in order to kill them. The practice is called takfīr, and Muslim scholars have been warning against it since the first civil war. Here are the facts of its origin.

After the battle of Siffin in 657, Ali agreed to arbitration with Muawiya. A group in his army objected, saying "judgement belongs to God alone,"[1] and left, hence their name, the Khawārij (those who went out). At Nahrawan in 658 Ali defeated them.

They then held that both sides had become unbelievers by agreeing to human arbitration.

In 661 a Kharijite named Ibn Muljam struck Ali with a poisoned sword as he went to the dawn prayer in Kufa. Ali died two days later. The group had claimed to act for God.

Their other marks were harshness toward Muslims who differed and a readiness to declare major sinners unbelievers.

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said that there would arise a people who recite the Quran "but it does not pass beyond their throats," who "pass through the religion as an arrow passes through its game," and whom those who fought them would be rewarded.[2] The early scholars applied the description to the Kharijites.

It was taken as a warning about piety without understanding.

The Prophet said: "If a man says to his brother 'O unbeliever,' it returns upon one of them."[3] The standard creed of Sunni Islam, the Tahawi creed, says "We do not declare any of the people of the qibla to be unbelievers for a sin, as long as he does not hold it lawful."[4]

So a Muslim is an unbeliever only by clear and deliberate rejection of what is certain in the faith, and only a court of qualified judges may so rule.

In 2004 the Amman Message, signed by hundreds of scholars from every school, affirmed the validity of eight schools of Islam and forbade declaring any of their followers apostates.[5] In 2014 an open letter of 126 scholars to the leader of ISIS condemned the group's takfir and violence. The Ibadis of Oman, who descend from the moderate wing of early Kharijism, reject extremism and follow tolerance.
