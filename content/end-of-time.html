Popular writing on Islamic end times mixes three layers: what the Quran says, what the hadith add, and what later tradition built on top. Most of the confusion comes from treating them as one. Here they are separated.

"They ask you about the Hour: when will it come? Say, its knowledge is with my Lord alone."[1] That is the Quran's position, and the Prophet repeated it. When Gabriel asked him about the Hour he answered, "The one asked knows no more than the one asking."[2]

This is the first test of any claim about dates. A prediction that the end will come in a given year contradicts the Quran.

The hadith do list signs, and they come in two kinds. The minor signs are social: people competing in building tall buildings, a loss of trust, a spread of ignorance.[3] The major signs are ten, among them the Dajjal, the return of Jesus, Gog and Magog, and the sun rising from the west.[4]

The signs are warnings to live carefully. The Prophet is not reported to have given them as a means of working out the date.

The Dajjal, "the deceiver", is not named in the Quran. He comes from the hadith, which describe a false claimant who is blind in one eye and who will tempt people with wonders.[5] The Prophet said that every prophet warned his people about him.[6]

The detail Muslims are taught is the test. His wonders are real, and the answer to him is faith and a recitation of the opening verses of Surah al-Kahf.[7] The figure stands for the final trial of belief against appearance.

Many Muslims expect a just leader from the Prophet's family before the end. The Quran does not mention the Mahdi. The reports come from hadith collections, in particular Abu Dawud and al-Tirmidhi, and scholars have graded them differently.[8] Some great scholars, among them Ibn Khaldun, doubted them.

So this is the one item on the list that a Muslim can reject or accept without leaving the faith. Belief in the Last Day is required. Belief in the Mahdi is a matter of the scholars' judgement of the reports.

"Who is there that can intercede with Him except by His permission?"[9] The Quran limits intercession at the root: nobody can plead with God unless God allows it, and then only for those He is pleased with.[10]

Within that limit, the Prophet said that on the Day of Judgement people will go to the prophets one by one asking for help, and each will excuse himself until they come to Muhammad, who will prostrate before God and then be told to ask.[11] It is a picture of mercy delivered by permission and not by right.
