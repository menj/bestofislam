Joseph Schacht, a twentieth-century Western scholar of Islamic law, argued that chains of transmission (isnads) for hadith, the reports of the words and acts of Muhammad (peace be upon him), grew backward over time. Critics repeat his conclusion in strong forms: oral for generations, compiled late, and mostly invented.

Below are five answers. Each states the objection at its strongest, concedes what is sound in it, and answers from the sources. The pattern is that some points are real, and the sweeping conclusions do not follow from them.

The objection begins with a fact: false hadith circulated. Political partisans and pious storytellers alike put sayings in the Prophet's mouth. Nobody disputes this, and Muslim scholars were the first to say it. Ibn Sirin, who died in 110 AH (about 728 CE), is reported to have said that after the first civil strife scholars began to ask transmitters to name their sources.[1] Abdullah ibn al-Mubarak is reported to have said that the isnad is part of the religion, and that without it anyone would say whatever he liked.[2]

A whole literature grew out of that concern: collections of fabricated reports such as Ibn al-Jawzi's Kitab al-Mawdu'at, and manuals that name the forgers and the motives behind them.[3] So the first point of the critique is accepted. The question left open is how much of the material survives scrutiny, and whether the scholars' own method for sorting it was sound. The next four entries take those questions in turn.

The claim of a century or more of purely oral transmission is the one most easily tested. Reports record written material from the start. The Prophet ordered that a sermon after the conquest of Mecca be written down for a man of Yemen named Abu Shah.[4] Ali is reported to have kept a sheet of rulings, and Abu Hurayra said that Abdullah ibn Amr wrote down more hadith than he did.[5] The text attributed to Abu Hurayra's student Hammam ibn Munabbih survives, and its contents appear in Ahmad ibn Hanbal's Musnad in the same wording.[6]

There is a real concession to make. Other reports record a prohibition on writing anything but the Quran, and early scholars disagreed about it.[7] Much teaching was oral, and memory carried a great load. But Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz is reported to have ordered the gathering of hadith around the year 100 AH,[8] and Malik's Muwatta was composed in Medina in the second century AH. The great collections of the third century came from many cities, among them Bukhara, Nishapur and Sijistan, and they do not point to one Baghdad project under political pressure.

The objection holds that chains are late inventions, attached to sayings to make them look old. If so, the science built around them would be an exercise in decoration. In practice the scholars of jarh wa ta'dil (criticism and praise of transmitters) compiled biographical dictionaries that record the birth, death, teachers, travels and reputation of thousands of narrators.[9] A narrator had to be upright and precise, and each link in a chain had to be able to have met the one before. A chain with a gap, or with a transmitter known to be careless, was graded weak.

They also tested the text itself (matn). A report that contradicts the Quran, a firmly established practice or an established fact was suspect however clean its chain looked.[10] Modern research has weighed in on this. Harald Motzki studied early collections such as the Musannaf of Abd al-Razzaq and argued that much of the legal material can be traced to the first century AH.[11] Other Western scholars remain more cautious, and Schacht's own case rested mainly on legal traditions. The honest summary is that the dating of individual reports is debated, and that the blanket claim of late invention has lost ground.

The strongest form of the argument says that chains improved over time, so a complete chain going to the Prophet is a sign of later invention. This has real support in one observation: a number of chains converge on a single transmitter, and some scholars, following Juynboll, read that point as the origin of the report, or its source of fabrication.[12]

The difficulty is the logic. If a chain is incomplete it is suspect, and if it is complete it is suspect, so no chain could ever count as evidence. A claim that nothing could confirm is a stipulation, not a finding. The Muslim scholars also never treated a clean chain as enough. They looked for the same report arriving by separate routes from different cities and different teachers, and they graded reports with a gap as weak by that very fact.[13] A forger would have to plant a matching text in several unconnected lines, and that is harder than planting one. Convergence on one transmitter may equally show a real collector who taught widely, which is how Motzki read it. For related discussion, see 5 Western scholars of Islam and where Muslims differ with them.

The number 600,000 usually comes from a report that al-Bukhari selected his book from about that many hadith.[14] It never meant 600,000 separate sayings that appeared from nowhere. Scholars counted each route as a separate hadith, so one saying with ten chains counted as ten. The Sahih itself holds a few thousand reports once the repeats are removed, and the exact count depends on the edition and the method of counting.[15]

Nor is the figure of 99.5 percent spurious a measured result. It follows from counting chains as sayings and from the premise in the previous entry. The scholars did reject most of what circulated, and that rejection shows the method working. Muslims also grade hadith unequally: a report ranked sound has a different standing from a weak one, and the Quran stands apart because it was transmitted by a different route and does not depend on any single chain. For how chains are used in a comparable case, see 5 things a Catholic Bible says about 2 Peter.

The bottom line

Schacht and his followers were right that hadith were forged, and Muslim scholars said so long before them. They were wrong, on the evidence, that writing began late, that chains were never tested, and that the figures quoted measure what they claim to measure.

The dating of particular reports remains a live debate among Muslim and Western scholars alike. That is a reason to examine each report on its merits, which is what the classical science of hadith set out to do.
