The Quran has Pharaoh's minister Haman overseeing construction and advising against Moses. The Bible has a minister named Haman too, except his king is Ahasuerus of Persia, and the Book of Esther puts him about 1,000 years after Moses.

Orientalists from the 17th century onward have called this a mix-up: Muhammad, they say, lifted a name from a story he half-remembered and dropped it into the wrong court. Here are 5 reasons that verdict moves faster than the evidence allows.

Two people can be called Haman without one being copied from the other. Adriaan Reland made this point in 1705, and it still holds: the argument runs from "the Quran mentions someone named Haman" to "therefore it means the Haman of Esther," with no step in between showing why those have to be the same person.

Names repeat. There were multiple Pharaohs, multiple Herods, multiple men named John in the New Testament alone. Treating a shared name as proof of borrowing would need to be argued, not assumed, and the critics mostly assume it.

The objection depends on lining up Moses with a fixed date and Esther with a later fixed date, then measuring the gap. The Quran does neither. It places Haman in Pharaoh's court during the Moses narrative and says nothing about when that court falls on a Persian or Babylonian calendar.

An anachronism is a claim placed at the wrong point in a timeline the text itself sets out. Import a timeline from outside the text, find a mismatch, and call it the text's error: that's the move being made here, and it's the reader's chronology doing the work, not the Quran's.

This is the strongest answer, and it rarely reaches the popular version of the objection. New Kingdom Egyptian inscriptions record the title ḥmn-ḥ, meaning overseer of the stone-quarry workers. A limestone door jamb now in the Kunsthistorisches Museum in Vienna, dated to the 19th Dynasty and likely the reign of Ramesses II, carries this exact title.

That's the period usually proposed for the Exodus, and it's a title for precisely the kind of construction authority the Quran assigns to Haman: a man Pharaoh puts in charge of building. A shared consonantal root behind an Egyptian title and the Quran's name for Pharaoh's builder is a real point of contact, not a coincidence has to explain away.

Pharaoh tells Haman to build a tower reaching toward the heavens. Critics read this as a second borrowing, this time from the Tower of Babel in Genesis, stitched onto the Esther mix-up to produce one confused composite.

Read plainly, it's simpler than that. Egyptian officialdom had a real class of men who ran monumental construction for the king, and Pharaoh in the Quran assigns exactly that job to exactly that kind of official. The story needs no second biblical source once the first is shown to be less solid than advertised.

Nöldeke, Mingana, Lammens, and the older Encyclopaedia of Islam entries all treat the Haman question as settled. The Encyclopaedia of the Quran, writing later, is more careful: it lists the Esther theory alongside the Egyptian-title theory and does not choose between them.

That shift matters. A claim confidently repeated for a century turns out, once the archaeology is checked, to rest on less than its confidence suggested. The word "Pharaoh" itself carries a comparable history: Egyptologists date its use as a royal title to the New Kingdom, which lines up with the Quran's Mosaic setting and does not line up with the earlier Joseph narrative in Genesis, an anachronism on the biblical side that gets far less attention.

The bottom line

The claim needs the two Hamans to be the same figure, needs a chronology the Quran doesn't supply, and needs to set aside a title recorded in Egyptian stone that matches the very office the Quran describes.

Take those three away and what's left is a name. Names are not a small thing to build a case on, but they are not enough of one either.
