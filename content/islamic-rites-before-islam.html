Critics of Islam often say that its rites were already old when Muhammad (peace be upon him) began to preach, and that he took them from the pagans of Mecca, from the Jews, or from a shrewd tribal chief. Part of that is true, and the Muslim sources themselves record it.

Below are six answers. Each states the objection as strongly as the evidence allows and then answers from the Quran, the hadith (reports of the Prophet's words and acts) and the early biographies. Where a report is weak or the evidence is thin, the text says so.

The objection says the Kaaba was a pile of stones, Mecca a cluster of mud huts, and Muhammad a participant in its rites. The first two points have a real basis. The Quran calls the valley one "without cultivation" (14:37), and Ibn Ishaq describes a low, roofless structure that Quraysh rebuilt when Muhammad was about 35. Ibn Ishaq adds that the new building had a timbered roof, so the claim of no wood does not match the account.[1][2]

Muhammad did take part in the rebuilding. Al-Bukhari reports him carrying stones, and Ibn Ishaq tells how the clans quarrelled over who would set the Black Stone until he laid it on a cloak that every clan lifted.[3] Muslims have always told this story, as evidence of his fairness before his call. The sources also say that he stood at Arafat with the pilgrims while the Quraysh stayed at Muzdalifah, and the Quran later told all pilgrims to move on from where the people move on (2:199).[4] For the stone itself, see 5 facts about the Black Stone and why Muslims touch it.

The objection says Abraham, Hagar and Ishmael at Mecca is impossible: the distance from Canaan is great, no settlement existed, and the accounts of Zamzam and of Safa and Marwa contradict one another. Begin with what the Quran says. It does not name Hagar or Zamzam. Abraham says he settled some of his offspring in a valley near the Sacred House (14:37), and he and Ishmael raise its foundations (2:127). Safa and Marwa are called signs of God (2:158). The full story comes from a long hadith in al-Bukhari, and the reports differ on details such as whether the spring came from the heel of the angel or the stroke of his wing.[1][5]

Two points weigh against the word impossible. The Hebrew Bible places Hagar and Ishmael in the wilderness of Paran (Genesis 21:21) and lists Ishmael's sons under Arabian names such as Kedar and Tema (Genesis 25:13-15).[6] Archaeology cannot confirm or refute any individual of that age, at Mecca or in Canaan; the sanctuary area has also seen almost no excavation. The Muslim claim rests on revelation and transmitted memory, and the silence of the ground does not decide it. For earlier outside witnesses, see The Kaaba before Islam: 5 ancient witnesses, weighed.

The objection says the genealogy from Ishmael to Muhammad is arithmetically impossible: about 15 generations spread over some 2,300 years. If that list were complete, the objection would hold. Muslim genealogists never claimed it was. Al-Bukhari's chapter on the lineage of the Prophet traces him up to Adnan and stops there. Ibn Ishaq gives longer lists between Adnan and Ishmael, and the lists disagree with each other.[7]

Many early scholars treated the line as sound as far as Adnan and uncertain beyond him, and some disliked tracing it further. Arabic ibn (son of) also means descendant, so a chain can skip generations, as biblical genealogies do. The Quran itself notes many unrecorded generations between earlier peoples (25:38).[8] The critic is right that the full chain cannot be dated or verified. No ritual or creed depends on a single name between Adnan and Ishmael, so the question is one for historians of Arabia and does not touch the faith.

The objection says Qusayy invented the hajj tax, the fairs, circumambulation, prostration and the Black Stone cult as a business, and that Muhammad renamed the result. The Muslim sources do credit Qusayy, a Quraysh leader of the fifth century, with real work: custody of the Kaaba, the water supply for pilgrims, and the rifada, a contribution levied on the Quraysh themselves to feed pilgrims.[9] So the levy is recorded, and it fell on the Quraysh. The sources also place fairs such as Ukaz in the season. Al-Bukhari reports that some Muslims hesitated to trade there, and the Quran allowed them to seek the bounty of their Lord (2:198).[10]

The same sources describe circumambulation, standing at Arafat and the rites at Mina as older than Qusayy, kept by Arabs as a legacy of Ishmael's line. They blame Amr ibn Luhayy for setting up idols.[11] At Arafat the Prophet told pilgrims to stay at their places, since they were heirs to the legacy of Abraham.[12] The Quran says Abraham proclaimed the pilgrimage (22:27). Whether Qusayy-era management shaped details of the rite cannot be tested beyond these accounts, but no Muslim source says the hajj began with him or with Muhammad.

The objection says these were taken from Jews and pagans, then altered to insult the Jews. Several are acknowledged in the sources. The month of Ramadan was in the Arabic calendar before Islam; the Quran says fasting was prescribed for earlier communities too (2:183-185).[13] Aisha reports that the Quraysh fasted Ashura (the tenth of Muharram) in the Jahiliyya, the time before Islam, and that Muhammad did so as well. In Medina he found Jews fasting it for the rescue of Moses and said Muslims had a better right to Moses. When the Ramadan fast was prescribed, Ashura became optional.[14] He later said he would add the ninth day.[15] No early source links either fast to Tammuz; that link rests on resemblance.

On Friday, the Quran commands the congregation (62:9), and a hadith in al-Bukhari says earlier communities were given a day of gathering, differed over it, and that God guided the Muslims to Friday. That report names the other communities without mockery.[16] For the qibla, see Jerusalem, then Mecca: 5 facts about the change of qibla. Tahannuth was the devotion Muhammad practised in the cave of Hira before the revelation, and Ibn Ishaq says the Quraysh practised it too.[17] It was a pre-Islamic habit of retreat, and it is no rite of Islamic law.

The Quran presents Islam as the religion of Abraham, a hanif (one who turned away from idols to God alone), and ties the rites to him (2:127-128; 3:67; 22:78).[18] Muslim sources name Arabs who held that line before Muhammad, among them Zayd ibn Amr ibn Nufayl, who is said to have told the Quraysh at the Kaaba that none of them followed the faith of Abraham.[19] A critic who sees continuity with Mecca's older practice is therefore describing what Islam claims. The dispute is over whether the older practice had been corrupted.

The sources list the changes. The idols around the Kaaba were removed at the conquest of Mecca (17:81).[20] Circumambulation without clothes was ended by a proclamation in the ninth year of the Hijra.[21] The Quraysh exemption from Arafat ended, and the pagan talbiya, which addressed partners of God, was replaced by a declaration of his oneness.[22] The Quran also condemned the practice of shifting the sacred months (9:36-37). For the rites as they stand, see 6 rites of Hajj and what they mean.

The bottom line

The Muslim sources already say much of what critics present as a discovery: Mecca was modest, the Quraysh rebuilt the Kaaba, Muhammad took part, the Quraysh fasted Ashura, and the Jews of Medina kept the same fast. The charge of concealment fails because the record is open.

What remains is the Muslim claim that these rites descend from Abraham, were corrupted over time and were restored. The evidence for it is transmitted testimony and revelation. Archaeology neither confirms nor refutes it, and a reader may weigh that as he sees fit. The claim that Qusayy invented the pilgrimage for profit has no support in the sources that report him.
