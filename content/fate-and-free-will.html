Every religion with an all-knowing God meets the same puzzle. If He knows what you will do, how can it be your choice? Muslim scholars took the question seriously for centuries, and they did not all agree. Here is what the sources say, and where the schools split.

Yes, and the Quran treats the two claims as compatible. "Whoever wishes, let him believe, and whoever wishes, let him disbelieve."[1] It also says that nobody wishes except as God wishes.[2]

The standard reply is about knowledge. Knowing is not forcing. A teacher who knows from years of experience which student will fail the exam has not made the student fail. God's knowledge is not bound by time as ours is, so what He knows about tomorrow is not a push from yesterday.

It does use the language of misguiding, and critics quote it. The verses are explained by their own wording. "He misleads none by it except the defiant."[3] The misguiding follows a person's choice of defiance. It is not the cause of it.

The Quran also says that God sends messengers "in the language of their people so that he may make clear to them," and then guides or misguides whom He wills.[4] Read together, the verses describe a God who offers guidance to all and who confirms a person in the direction he has already chosen.

The Quran's answer is that life is an examination. "He created death and life to test which of you is best in deed."[5] A test with no possibility of failure is not a test.

The Prophet's own prayer is careful with the point. He used to say, "All good is in Your hands, and evil is not attributed to You."[6] Scholars take this to mean that God permits evil as the cost of freedom and does not will it as an end. The Quran also holds people responsible for what they do to each other: "Corruption has appeared on land and sea because of what people's hands have earned."[7]

There were three main answers. The Jabriyya said that people are compelled and their acts are God's alone. The Qadariyya and the Mu'tazila said that people create their own acts, and that justice requires it. Neither extreme won.

Al-Ash'ari (died 935) took the middle path that most Sunnis follow. God creates every act, and the person "acquires" it, meaning he chooses and owns it, and is accountable.[8] The Maturidi school, followed by the Hanafis, put more weight on human choice. Faruqi, writing on the same debate about the divine attributes, praised al-Ash'ari for refusing both extremes and for answering the "how" of God's action with bilā kayf, "without asking how", the position that the Quran's statements are true as they stand and cannot be pressed into human categories.[9]

The record says no. When Umar was told about a plague in Syria he turned back, and a companion asked, "Are you fleeing from God's decree?" He answered, "Yes, we flee from God's decree to God's decree."[10] The Prophet's instruction to a man who left his camel unattended was "Tie it, and then rely on God."[11]

Trust and effort go together. A Muslim plans, works and takes medicine, and then accepts what comes. The Quran's phrase for the second half is tawakkul, reliance on God, and it follows action. It does not replace it.
