Every religion with an all-knowing God meets the same puzzle. If He knows what you will do, how can it be your choice? Muslim scholars took the question seriously for centuries, and they did not all agree.

Belief in qadar, God's decree, is one of the six articles of faith that the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, taught in the hadith of Gabriel (hadith: a report of the words and deeds of the Prophet).[1] Here is what the sources say about it, how the schools divided, and what the doctrine does to the way a Muslim lives.

Yes, and the Quran treats the two claims as compatible. "Whoever wishes, let him believe, and whoever wishes, let him disbelieve."[2] It also says that nobody wishes except as God wishes.[3]

The standard reply is about knowledge. Knowing is not forcing. A teacher who knows from years of experience which student will fail the exam has not made the student fail. God's knowledge is not bound by time as ours is, so what He knows about tomorrow is not a push from yesterday.

The strongest objection is that the analogy is weak, because a teacher guesses and God knows with certainty. If the outcome is certain, the student cannot do otherwise. Muslim theologians answered that the certainty follows the act and does not precede it as a cause. God knows the choice because the person will make it, and the person makes it as a real agent. The first verse above holds a person responsible for the choice, and the second places that choice within God's wider will.

It does use the language of misguiding, and critics quote it. The verses are explained by their own wording. "He misleads none by it except the defiant."[4] The misguiding follows a person's choice of defiance. It is not the cause of it.

The same pattern holds where the Quran speaks of sealed hearts. One verse says that God has sealed them, and the words that follow give the reason: "because of their disbelief". Another says that when they turned aside, God turned their hearts aside.[5] In both, the hardening is a response to a choice already made.

The Quran also says that God sends messengers "in the language of their people so that he may make clear to them," and then guides or misguides whom He wills.[6] Read together, the verses describe a God who offers guidance to all and who confirms a person in the direction he has already chosen.

The Quran's answer is that life is an examination. "He created death and life to test which of you is best in deed."[7] A test with no possibility of failure is not a test.

The Prophet's own prayer is careful with the point. He used to say, "All good is in Your hands, and evil is not attributed to You."[8] Scholars take this to mean that God permits evil as the cost of freedom and does not will it as an end. The Quran also holds people responsible for what they do to each other: "Corruption has appeared on land and sea because of what people's hands have earned."[9]

One verse says that whatever good befalls a person is from God, and whatever evil befalls him is from himself.[10] The verse before it says that everything is from God, and the commentators joined the two by distinguishing what God decrees from what a person does with it. Muslim theologians do not claim to have removed the difficulty. They hold that God's wisdom exceeds human measure.

There were three main answers. The Jabriyya, associated with Jahm ibn Safwan (died 746), said that people are compelled and their acts are God's alone. The Qadariyya and the Mu'tazila said that people create their own acts, and that justice requires it, since God would be unjust to punish what He had Himself made them do. Neither extreme won.

Al-Ash'ari (died 935) took the middle path that most Sunnis follow. God creates every act, and the person "acquires" it, meaning he chooses and owns it, and is accountable.[11] The Maturidi school, founded by al-Maturidi of Samarkand (died 944) and followed by the Hanafis, put more weight on human choice. Faruqi, writing on the same debate about the divine attributes, praised al-Ash'ari for refusing both extremes and for answering the "how" of God's action with bilā kayf, "without asking how", the position that the Quran's statements are true as they stand and cannot be pressed into human categories.[12]

Critics call "acquisition" a verbal compromise. Defenders reply that it names a real distinction between creating an act and owning it, and it keeps both God's power and human responsibility.

The record says no. When Umar was told about a plague in Syria he turned back, and a companion asked, "Are you fleeing from God's decree?" He answered, "Yes, we flee from God's decree to God's decree."[13] The Prophet's instruction to a man who left his camel unattended was "Tie it, and then rely on God."[14]

Trust and effort go together. A Muslim plans, works and takes medicine, and then accepts what comes. The Quran's phrase for the second half is tawakkul, reliance on God, and it follows action. It does not replace it.

When the Companions asked whether they should rely on what was already written, the Prophet told them to act, because each person is made ready for what he was created for.[15] The Quran also rebukes those who plead the decree as an excuse for their wrongdoing.[16] Its instruction for a decision is to resolve the matter, and then to rely on God.[17]

The bottom line

The sources hold two things together. God's knowledge and will are complete, and people choose their acts and answer for them. The Quran addresses its readers as agents who can believe or disbelieve, and it says in the same breath that nothing happens outside God's will.

Muslim scholars did not agree on how the two fit, and the schools that tried to resolve the tension in either direction lost to the middle position. The difficulty is real, and honest Muslim writers say so. The practical teaching leaves no doubt: act, plan and take responsibility, and accept the outcome with trust in God.
