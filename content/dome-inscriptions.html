The Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem was built for the Umayyad caliph ʿAbd al-Malik and carries a foundation date of 72 AH, which is 691 to 692 CE. Its inscriptions run in two long bands of blue and gold glass mosaic around the inner and outer faces of its octagonal arcade, with two more on copper plaques over its eastern and northern doors.[1]

They are among the earliest dated texts in Arabic that quote the Quran, set up about sixty years after the Prophet's death. Here is what they show.

More than a century after the building went up, the ʿAbbasid caliph al-Maʾmun, who reigned from 813 to 833, had his own name set into the foundation notice in place of ʿAbd al-Malik's. His craftsmen left the date untouched.[1]

That oversight is a gift to historians. A caliph who died in 833 could not have built a dome dated 72 AH, so the substitution itself proves that the inscriptions belong to ʿAbd al-Malik's reign.

The mosaic bands quote whole passages that stand in the Quran now: the verses addressing the People of the Book in the fourth surah (sūrah, "chapter"), the verses on Jesus in the nineteenth, the witness to God's oneness in the third, the whole of the one hundred and twelfth, and more.[2]

The historian Estelle Whelan, in the fullest study of the inscriptions, found that with minor variations the passages reflect the standard printed text of Cairo.[1] The variations are of a known kind: a change of person, a phrase omitted, two passages joined to fit the sense. Islamic inscriptions of every later century do the same, long after no one doubts that the text was fixed.[1]

The main inscription, on the inner face of the arcade, speaks to one audience. It opens with the declaration of faith (shahādah, "witness") and then quotes the Quran's words to the People of the Book: "The Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, was only a messenger of God", and "say not 'Three'".[1]

Whelan describes its single theme as a challenge to Christian dogma in the main Christian pilgrimage city.[1] The Quran was already the text a caliph reached for when he wished to state what Islam believes about Jesus, in public and in permanent form.

The plaques over the doors tell the same story on a smaller scale. Seven lines of the original text survive on one and four on the other; the rest of each was covered by a new sheet of copper inscribed in al-Maʾmun's name.[1] The replacement over the eastern door is dated to the month of Rabīʿ al-Ākhir in the year 216, in 831 CE.[3]

The surviving lines are original. They open with the phrase that begins every surah but one (basmalah, "In the name of God"), and they echo the Quran's one hundred and twelfth surah: "He begetteth not nor was begotten".[1]

The historians Patricia Crone and Michael Cook accepted that the mosaics agree extensively with the Quran, but they pointed to "extensive deviance" in the copper plaques as a reason to doubt that the text was settled.[4]

Whelan answered that one plaque is not primarily Quranic in character, and that the other weaves Quranic fragments and paraphrases together in a way that makes sense only as the handling of a text everyone recognised. A preacher who alludes to scripture assumes that his hearers know it. On her reading, the Quran was already the common property of the community in the last decade of the seventh century.[1]

When ʿAbd al-Malik's son al-Walid rebuilt the Prophet's mosque at Madinah, between 706 and 710, its wall facing the direction of prayer (qiblah, "direction of Mecca") was inscribed with the Quran's first surah and then its last twenty-four, from the ninety-first to the one hundred and fourteenth. A traveller described the inscription in 903, and a second visitor confirmed it between 920 and 929.[1]

That shows the order of the short surahs already fixed by 710, including the first and the last two. Whelan's conclusion from the whole body of evidence is that the Muslim tradition is reliable, at least in broad outline, in attributing the first official copy of the Quran to ʿUthman and his commission.[1]

The bottom line

The Dome of the Rock shows a text already fixed and familiar within sixty years of the Prophet's death: quoted in public, adapted for a message, and recognised by the people who read it. The date of the first written copy is a question for the manuscripts.

That agrees with the earliest manuscripts, which the site examines in its pieces on the Birmingham leaves and on ʿUthman's copies.
