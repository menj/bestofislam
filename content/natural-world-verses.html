The Quran refers to nature constantly, but as a sign and not as a lesson in science. The five verses below are often quoted. Each has a classical reading and a modern one, and a careful reader should know both.

"We have adorned the lowest heaven with lamps, and made them missiles against the devils."[1] Surah 72 has the jinn say that they once sat in places to listen, and now find a guard of fierce warriors and flaming fires.[2]

Classical commentators read it as the stars (or meteors) being used to drive away eavesdropping devils. Some modern writers read it as figurative. What is common to all is the Quran's point, that the unseen is not open to anyone's hearing without God's permission.

"Have We not made the earth a resting place, and the mountains as pegs?"[3] The word awtād is tent pegs. The classical sense is that mountains steady the earth against shaking, as the Quran says in another place.[4]

Some modern writers connect it to the roots of mountains found by geology. Others warn that the word is used as a picture, and that neither reading should be built into a doctrine.

"By the fig and the olive, and Mount Sinai, and this secure city."[5] The oaths are by places and plants of revelation: the fig and olive of Syria and Palestine, Sinai where Moses was spoken to, and Mecca. The verse is not a statement on diet or botany.

Some say the oath is by the places where the fig and olive grow, which are also the lands of the prophets. That is the view most commentators take.

Surah 99, al-Zalzala, begins "When the earth is shaken with its final quaking." It describes the Last Day and gives the line, "Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it."[6] Faruqi reports that the orientalist H. A. R. Gibb said he felt the earth shaking under his feet as he recited it.[8]

It is not a prediction of earthquakes, and the Quran does not try to teach seismology. It uses a natural terror to place the reader before the final accounting.

"He let loose the two seas, meeting each other, with a barrier between them which they do not cross."[7] Some writers say this describes the meeting of the sea and fresh water at an estuary, where the waters keep their character for a stretch.

Classical commentators generally took it as two bodies of water, salt and fresh, kept apart by God's decree. This is another case where the verse describes an observable thing in a few words, and the modern claims go beyond what the verse says.
