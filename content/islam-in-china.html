China has more than twenty million Muslims, and a Muslim presence that goes back to the century after the Prophet. It is one of the oldest and least known Muslim communities.

Chinese records mention an Arab embassy to the Tang court in 651, sent by the third caliph, Uthman. The Huaisheng Mosque in Guangzhou is traditionally credited to a Companion, Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas, though the date and the founder are disputed by historians.

Arab and Persian traders came by sea to Guangzhou and Quanzhou and by land along the Silk Road, and many settled and married locally.

The Hui, about ten million people, are Chinese in language and culture and Muslim in religion. They live in every province, most densely in Ningxia and Gansu. They descend from Arab and Persian traders and soldiers and from Chinese converts. The name Huí came to be applied to Chinese Muslims in general.

Alongside them are Turkic groups, among them the Uyghurs, Kazakhs and Kyrgyz, who live mainly in the northwest.

The Great Mosque of Xi'an, founded according to tradition in 742, stands in the form of a Chinese temple, with courtyards, pavilions and a pagoda-style minaret. Arabic calligraphy hangs in Chinese-style frames.

This fits the Islamic principle that the form of a mosque is not fixed. The scholar Ismail al-Faruqi argued that the Muslim artist's task was to avoid portraying creation as divine and to use pattern and calligraphy to point beyond it, and that principle, rather than a particular style, is what makes a building Islamic.[1] Chinese mosques follow it in a Chinese idiom.

Zheng He (1371–1433), the Ming admiral who led seven great voyages to Southeast Asia, India, Arabia and East Africa, was born Ma He into a Muslim family in Yunnan. His fleets reached Arabia and East Africa, and some of his envoys are recorded as having made the pilgrimage to Mecca.

His story is often cited as evidence that Muslims held high office in imperial China.

Du Wenxiu (1823–1872), a Hui of Yunnan, led a rebellion against Qing rule that began in 1856 and founded a state at Dali with himself as Sultan Sulayman. It lasted until the Qing crushed it in 1872. The rebellion followed massacres of Muslims and grew out of local conflict between Hui and Han.

The Burmese called the rebels Panthay. Du Wenxiu is remembered in China, in different ways, as a rebel leader and as a defender of his people.
