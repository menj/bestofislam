Popular Muslim writing is full of claims that the Quran anticipated modern science. Some are interesting. Some are weak, and repeating them damages the faith they are meant to support. This article grades seven, from strongest to weakest, and says why.

A word first about the Quran's own claim. The scholar Ismail al-Faruqi argued that the "miracle" the Quran offers is its language: the challenge to produce a chapter like it (2:23) is addressed to the intellect of those who know Arabic, and it is a different kind of sign from the parting of a sea, which overwhelms the mind.[1] On that view science is not the Quran's proof, and a weak scientific claim costs nothing the faith depends on.

"Do you not see that God sends down water from the sky and leads it through the earth as springs?"[2] "God is who sends the winds that raise clouds, and spreads them in the sky as He wills, and you see the rain come out of them."[3] The verses describe evaporation, clouds, rain and groundwater accurately. Grade: sound.

The grade stops there because the description was within reach of ancient observers. The Hebrew Bible says something similar (Ecclesiastes 1:7, where the rivers run to the sea and return to the place they came from), and Aristotle set out an account of evaporation and rainfall in his Meteorologica.[4] A sceptic will say the verses therefore prove nothing. That is correct if the verses are offered as a miracle, and the claim here is more modest: the Quran uses an accurate picture of the world as a sign of God's provision, and nothing in it has been overturned.

The Quran describes human creation from a drop (nuṭfa), then a clinging thing (ʿalaqa), then a chewed lump (muḍgha), then bones clothed in flesh.[5] Embryologists such as Keith Moore, in a later edition of his textbook prepared with Abdul Majeed al-Zindani, reported close parallels, and Maurice Bucaille drew attention to them.[6]

Critics note that Galen and others had described stages, and that the terms can be read many ways: ʿalaqa has been taken as a clot, a leech-like thing, or something that clings. They also point out that the order "bones, then flesh" does not match modern embryology, in which bone and muscle develop together. Defenders answer that the Arabic describes appearance and function and not a laboratory sequence. Grade: interesting, not decisive. The sequence is a good starting point for a conversation, not a proof, because the match depends on how the terms are translated and on a textbook edition that was itself written with an interest in the result.

"It is not for the sun to overtake the moon, nor does the night outstrip the day. Each swims in an orbit."[7] The verse says that celestial bodies move on their own courses, which fits modern astronomy. A parallel verse, 21:33, says the same of the night, the day, the sun and the moon. Grade: fair.

The word falak (orbit, sphere) was also used in older cosmologies, so the verse does not teach a new model. Classical astronomers working within the Ptolemaic system took it to refer to the sphere or track of each body, and no commentator found a conflict with a sun and moon that circle the earth. A modern reader finds a fit with the heliocentric solar system, which the wording allows, but the wording is general. What the verse says plainly is that nothing in the sky moves at random, and that point holds under either model.

"An ant said, 'O ants, enter your dwellings.'"[8] Ants do communicate by chemical signals and touch, and the story fits that. Grade: modest. The claim that the Quran knew it is not needed, since people observed ants in the ancient world.

The verse sits inside the story of Solomon, who had been taught the speech of the birds, and it records that he understood the ant and smiled. The marvel in the passage is therefore a gift given to one prophet, and the modern finding about pheromones is a side observation about the real animal behind it. Anyone who has watched a column of ants part and re-form around an obstacle will recognise the picture without any laboratory.

"Not even the weight of an atom (dharra) is hidden from your Lord."[9] The Arabic word means a small ant or a speck of dust. Reading it as the subatomic particle is a modern layer. Grade: weak.

Early commentators glossed dharra as a tiny ant or a mote seen in a beam of sunlight, and the phrase "weight of a dharra" is used again in 99:7 for a deed of the smallest size. The idea of an indivisible atom was also old: Greek thinkers such as Democritus held it, and Muslim theologians of the kalām tradition developed their own atomism. The word could not have pointed to the physicist's atom, which can be split. The verse's point, that nothing escapes God's knowledge, needs no physics, and loses force when it is made to carry a claim it never made.

The Quran says God can "put together the very tips of his fingers."[10] The word is banān (fingertips). The claim that this refers to fingerprints is repeated often. Grade: weak. The verse is about God's power to remake a person and does not mention uniqueness of prints.

The context shows why. The passage asks whether a person thinks God will not gather his bones on the Day of Resurrection, and the answer is that God is able to restore even the fingertips, the smallest and finest part of the body. The point is the completeness of the restoration. The uniqueness of prints was recognised in the modern period, with Francis Galton's study of 1892 setting out the evidence, and nothing in the verse or in its classical commentary speaks of lines or patterns.

"We sent down iron, in which is great strength."[11] Some write that this means iron came from space. Grade: weak. The same Arabic verb is used of livestock (39:6) and clothing (7:26), where it plainly means "provided".

Supporters reply that iron nuclei are formed in stars and reach the earth in meteorites and dust, so the verb is literally true. The fact about iron is sound. The difficulty is that the same reasoning would make cattle and clothing fall from the sky, and the verse says what the iron is for: it carries "great strength" and benefits for people, and it appears in a sentence about the Book and the balance sent with the messengers. The sense is that God provided iron as a resource, and the astronomy adds an observation the verse does not state.

The bottom line

The grades fall in a clear order. The water cycle and orbits describe nature correctly, the embryology verses invite discussion, and the claims about atoms, fingerprints and iron depend on stretching a word past its meaning. None of the seven is a proof that the Quran came from God, and the strongest are accurate observations that ancient peoples also made.

That is a safer position for a Muslim than the long list. A claim tied to the science of one decade fails when the science moves, and the faith then looks as if it failed with it. The Andalusian philosopher Ibn Rushd had already advised that where demonstrated knowledge seems to conflict with the plain wording of a verse, the verse is to be read figuratively.[12] The Quran invites reflection on the creation, and it is best read for the questions it raises about the world.
