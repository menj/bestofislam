Few charges against Islam are repeated as often as this one: that it punishes anyone who leaves it with death. Critics cite classical law and opinion surveys; defenders cite the Quran's words on freedom of belief.

Both are citing real evidence. Here are six facts about apostasy (riddah, "turning back" from the faith), with the difficult ones stated first.

The honest place to begin is the law. The mainstream position in Islamic jurisprudence holds that a Muslim who leaves the faith is to be put to death, and many scholars teach it today; some hold that the penalty applies whether or not the apostate takes up arms against Muslims.[1]

Any account that denies this will not survive its first encounter with a critic who has read the law books.

The ruling rests on the reports of the Prophet's words. The best known is recorded by al-Bukhari: when the caliph ʿAlī burned a group of heretics, Ibn ʿAbbās objected that the Prophet had forbidden punishment by fire, and said he would instead have applied the Prophet's words, "Whoever changed his Islamic religion, then kill him."[2]

A second report, recorded by both al-Bukhari and Muslim, permits taking a Muslim's life in three cases only, one of them "the deserter of his Din (Islam), abandoning the community".[3]

The Quran speaks of apostasy several times. It warns that one who turns back and "dies while he is a disbeliever" has lost his deeds in this world and the next; it promises that if believers turn back, God will bring another people in their place; and it threatens those who disbelieve after belief with God's wrath, while excusing anyone forced to renounce the faith whose heart remains firm.[4]

Every one of these passages places the punishment with God, in the hereafter. One even describes people who "believed then disbelieved, then believed, then disbelieved", which assumes they lived on after turning away.[5] And the Quran states as a principle that "there shall be no compulsion in [acceptance of] the religion".[6]

The report of the three cases joins two things: leaving the faith and abandoning the community (jamāʿah, the body of believers). Mainstream jurists read the two phrases as describing one act. A minority of modern scholars read them as two conditions, and hold that the penalty was never meant for a change of belief alone, only for apostasy that amounted to treason or rebellion against the Muslim polity.[1][7]

On that reading, the hadith addresses a political crime, closer to treason than to a change of belief.

In 2007 Ali Gomaa, then Grand Mufti of Egypt, wrote that a Muslim can choose a religion other than Islam. Leaving the faith is a grave sin that God will judge on the Day of Judgment, he wrote, and where the case is one of rejecting faith and nothing more, there is no worldly punishment.[8]

His statement was fiercely criticised by conservative scholars, and he issued a clarification: in it he reaffirmed freedom of belief, while holding that apostasy joined to active subversion of society remains a matter for the courts.[9] The episode shows both the view and the resistance to it.

In 2013 the Pew Research Center asked Muslims in many countries about sharia. Only those who first said they wanted it as their country's official law were then asked about executing apostates. Among those supporters, backing for the death penalty ranged from 86 percent in Egypt and 79 percent in Afghanistan to 44 percent in Bangladesh and 18 percent in Indonesia.[10]

Those figures are often quoted as though they described all Muslims in each country, which overstates them. They still show substantial support in several countries, and Muslims should face that plainly.

The bottom line

Mainstream Islamic law prescribes death for apostasy, on the strength of the hadith; the Quran condemns apostasy while placing its punishment in the hereafter, and insists that there is no compulsion in religion.

Muslim scholars today are divided between those who uphold the classical ruling and those who confine it to treason, and a former Grand Mufti of Egypt is among those who have taken the second view. That is the debate as it stands, and it is a debate among Muslims.
