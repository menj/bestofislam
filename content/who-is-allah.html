Most confusion about Islam starts here, with a name. So let's deal with the name first.

Allāh is the Arabic word for God. Arabic-speaking Christians use it. Arabic Bibles use it. The Maltese, whose language descends from Arabic, call God Alla in church.

It isn't a name in the sense that Zeus is a name. It's the definite noun: al-ilāh, the god, contracted. When a Muslim says Allah, the claim is that this is the God of Abraham, Moses and Jesus, the one the Quran says sent them all.

You can argue that Muslims and Christians describe God differently, and they do. That's a different argument from saying they mean different beings. A Jew and a Christian describe God differently too.

This one circulates on the internet with a diagram of a crescent. It rests on 2 facts and a leap. Pre-Islamic Arabia had various deities. Some crescent imagery appears on later Islamic buildings. Therefore, Allah was originally a lunar deity.

The leap is the problem. The Quran names the sun and moon as created things, and forbids worshipping either.[1] The crescent as a symbol of Islam is Ottoman, 700 years after Muhammad. And Muhammad's own father was named ʿAbd Allāh, meaning servant of God, which tells you the word was in use for the high God of Arabia before Islam existed, not for a moon.

The impression comes from the emphasis on transcendence: God is unlike anything, beyond imagining, not to be depicted.

Read the Quran's own vocabulary. Of the 99 names by which God is described in the tradition, the 2 that open nearly every chapter are al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm, both from the root for mercy and the womb. God is described as nearer to a person than his jugular vein.[2] The hadith literature has God saying he comes to the one who walks towards him at a run.

Transcendent and near aren't opposites in Islam. They're both asserted, and the tension between them is where the theology lives.

The names are often presented as a list to memorise, and there is a tradition that memorising them has merit.

The point of them is different. Each is a lens. The Provider, the Just, the Forgiving, the Subtle, the Withholder, the Reckoner. Read together, they describe a God who is not one thing, and who is not reducible to the single attribute a critic wants to argue against. Someone who says Islam's God is only a judge has read 1 name out of 99.

Nobody who has heard the declaration of faith would think this, since it puts Muhammad second and calls him a messenger. But the impression persists, partly from the honour Muslims show him.

The line is sharp in practice. Muhammad is not prayed to. He's not depicted. He's described in the Quran as a man who will die, who can't see the unseen, who has no power to help or harm anyone.[3] The reverence is for the messenger. The worship is for the one who sent him, and confusing the 2 is the single thing Islam is most concerned to prevent.

The bottom line

Allah is the Arabic word for God, used by Muslims and Arab Christians alike. Not a moon god, not a stranger, not a judge alone, and not Muhammad.

Every other question about Islam gets easier once that one is settled.
