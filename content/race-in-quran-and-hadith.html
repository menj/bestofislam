Critics say that the Quran and the hadith (reports of the words and actions of Muhammad (peace be upon him)) teach contempt for black people. They point to a curse on the descendants of Noah, to praise of the Arabs as the most noble, and to a few reports that speak harshly of dark skin.

Some of those reports exist, and some later medieval writings do contain racial prejudice. Below are five points that keep apart the Quran, authentic reports, weak reports and traditions of Israelite origin (isra'iliyyat), and that set the counter-evidence beside each charge.

The charge that the Quran ranks peoples meets its hardest test at Quran 49:13. The verse addresses all humankind, says that God made them into peoples and tribes so that they might know one another, and ends: the most noble of you in the sight of God is the most righteous.[1] Nobility is a matter of conduct. Quran 30:22 lists the variety of languages and colours among the signs of God, in the same sentence as the creation of the heavens and the earth.[2]

Two claims in the critique have no basis in the Quran. It names no curse on a son of Noah and says nothing about skin; its one story of Noah's family concerns a son who refused the ark (11:42-43).[3] And no verse calls the Arabs the most noble people. Hadith do record that leadership of the community belonged to Quraysh, the tribe of the Prophet, in his day.[4] That was a statement about tribal and political standing. Later writers sometimes stretched it into a claim of ethnic superiority, and that stretch is a fair target for criticism.

In the sermon he gave near the end of his life, Muhammad said that an Arab has no superiority over a non-Arab, nor a non-Arab over an Arab, nor a white over a black, nor a black over a white, except through taqwa (God-consciousness).[5] The report is in the Musnad of Ahmad, and its chain goes back to a man who heard the sermon. A number of hadith scholars grade it sound, and a reader should check the grading in the edition used.

Bukhari records a quarrel in which the Companion Abu Dharr insulted another man through his mother. The Prophet told him that he was a man with some of the ignorance of the pre-Islamic age (jahiliyya) still in him, and that those under a man's hand are his brothers, to be fed and clothed as he is.[6] Some versions name the man insulted as Bilal. The lesson of the report holds either way: the Prophet treated an insult based on descent or colour as a fault to be corrected.

The pilgrimage enacts the same teaching. Ismail al Faruqi calls the hajj the clearest embodiment of Islam's egalitarianism, with pilgrims of every race and class standing in the same plain dress; it was this sight, he notes, that persuaded Malcolm X to give up black racialism.[7]

Bilal was an Abyssinian slave who was tortured for his faith in Mecca and freed by Abu Bakr. He became the first muadhdhin, the one who gives the call to prayer. Umar later called Abu Bakr "our master" who had freed "our master", meaning Bilal.[8] When the first Muslims fled persecution, the Prophet sent them to the Christian king of Abyssinia, who sheltered them. When that king, the Negus, died, Muhammad told the Muslims of his death and led the funeral prayer for him in absentia.[9]

Usama ibn Zayd was the son of a freed slave. In the Prophet's last illness he appointed Usama to command an army that included senior Companions. Some people criticised the choice, and the Prophet defended it.[10] Salman, a Persian, came to Medina as a seeker and an enslaved man, and later advised the digging of the trench that protected the city.[11] None of this proves that no Muslim ever held a prejudice. It shows that the first community gave authority, honour and trust to people of African and Persian origin.

Bukhari has a saying that the Prophet's followers must listen and obey even if an Abyssinian slave "whose head is like a raisin" is placed in authority over them.[12] The report is authentic, and the physical simile may jar a modern reader. Its point is about obedience to lawful authority: the example was chosen as the lowest social position imaginable, so that no origin could disqualify a ruler.

The well-known report with "dinar" in it is the saying "wretched is the slave of the dinar and the dirham", in Bukhari.[13] The Arabic word abd here means a servant or devotee, and the target is the person who serves money. I know of no word for black in it. A critic's wording, "negro slave of Dinar", has to be checked against the primary text before it is accepted.

The saying about Nabtal comes from the biographical literature on the Prophet, not from the major hadith collections.[14] Its wording, its chain and its grade must be checked in the primary text. Reports of that kind do not carry the standing of an authentic hadith. In the account critics give, the target is one named man and not a people.

The story that Noah cursed Ham and condemned his line to servitude comes from the Bible, where the curse falls on Ham's son Canaan, and no word of skin colour appears.[15] Some early Muslim historians, al-Tabari among them, passed on material from earlier traditions about the sons of Noah.[16] Muslim scholars call such material isra'iliyyat and treat it as unverified, since it did not come from the Prophet. A report in al-Tirmidhi on the descent of peoples from Noah's sons is graded weak by many.[17]

Medieval prejudice was real, though. Ibn Khaldun called the Ham story baseless and explained skin colour by climate, yet in the same book he wrote demeaning things about black peoples.[18] Against that stands al-Jahiz, who wrote a defence of the Zanj, the black peoples of East Africa, against those who looked down on them.[19] Scholars of the tradition were divided, and their prejudices came from their own societies. Those prejudices have no warrant in the Quran or in the sound reports.

The bottom line

The Quran ranks people by righteousness and names no curse on any race. The farewell sermon says so in plain words, and the lives of Bilal, Usama, Salman and the Negus show a community that honoured people of every origin. Several reports critics quote are either graded weak, drawn from Israelite sources, or misread.

The honest concession is that some medieval Muslim writers shared the prejudices of their age and wrote them down. The remedy the tradition itself supplies is to test every such claim against the Quran and the sound reports.
