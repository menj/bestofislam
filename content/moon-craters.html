The names on the Moon read like a roll of honour for the history of astronomy. When the International Astronomical Union formally adopted the lunar names in 1935, a striking number of them belonged to astronomers who had worked in Arabic, most of them under the Latin names by which medieval Europe knew them.[1]

Here are 6 of them, and what each one did to earn a place in the sky.

A large walled plain near the centre of the Moon's visible face carries the Latin name of al-Battani, who worked at Raqqa and died in 929.[1] His measurement of the solar year was remarkably close to the modern value, and his figures for the tilt of the Earth's axis and the slow drift of the equinoxes served astronomers for centuries.[2]

His tables reached Latin Europe in a translation made in the 1130s and were printed in 1537. Their accuracy encouraged Copernicus, and Tycho Brahe, Kepler, Galileo and Halley all worked with his observations.[2] He also replaced the Greek geometrical methods with the sines and tangents of trigonometry.

A mountain-walled crater in the southern highlands is named for ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī, a Persian astronomer of the 10th century.[3] His great work set out the stars constellation by constellation, recording the position, brightness and colour of each one, with every constellation drawn twice: once as it would appear from outside a celestial globe, and once from inside it.[4]

Arzachel honours al-Zarqālī of Toledo, who prepared the astronomical tables named after his city.[5] His work may have influenced Copernicus.[5]

Near the Moon's eastern edge, beside the dark plain of Mare Crisium, lies a crater named for Ibn al-Haytham, the Arab scientist whom the Latin West knew as Alhazen.[6] The name was formally adopted in 1935.

His crater appears oblong from Earth only because it sits so close to the edge of the visible disc. Seen from above, its rim is nearly circular.[6]

Not every name was visible from Earth. Al-Biruni lies on the Moon's far side, just beyond the eastern limb, and honours the astronomer, mathematician and geographer who worked in Afghanistan and India and died around 1048.[7]

He used the timings of lunar eclipses to work out the difference in longitude between cities, and he estimated the radius of the Earth by measuring the dip of the horizon from a mountain, applying spherical trigonometry centuries before Europe systematised it.[8]

One crater honours a patron. Almanon is named for the caliph al-Maʾmūn, son of Hārūn al-Rashīd, who sponsored the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, where translators and astronomers worked side by side.[3] Al-Farghānī, whose own crater Alfraganus lies not far away, was one of the astronomers in his circle.

The bottom line

The Moon's map was drawn by astronomers who knew exactly whose work they had inherited. Latin Europe learned its astronomy from these men, often before it learned their real names.

Look up at a half Moon on a clear night. Near the line between light and shadow, the craters of al-Battani and al-Sufi may be catching the light.
