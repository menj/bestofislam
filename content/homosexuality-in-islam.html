This is among the questions on which Islamic teaching is traditional and plain, and on which the treatment of people has often fallen short of it. A fair account sets out both.

The Quran tells of Lot's people, who "approach men with desire instead of women" and were destroyed.[1] It calls the act an indecency (fāḥisha). The tradition has understood sexual relations as lawful only within marriage between a man and a woman.

There is no punishment in the Quran for the act. The jurists differed. The Hanafis held that no fixed penalty applies, and left it to the judge's discretion. Others compared it to adultery, and some prescribed death on the strength of a hadith that many scholars consider weak.[2]

If it were treated as adultery, the proof is four eyewitnesses to the act itself.[3] The Quran forbids spying on people: "Do not spy."[4] The Prophet told people not to expose what God had concealed. In practice, the standard of proof made a conviction almost impossible without a confession.

Scholars distinguish between what a person feels and what he does. The Quran says "God does not burden a soul beyond its capacity."[5] A person who is tested with an inclination and who refrains is, in the tradition's terms, like anyone who restrains a desire for the sake of God. This is a teaching about conduct, and not a claim about the origin of the inclination.

Vigilante violence, torture or killing of people by individuals has no basis in Islamic law, which reserves punishment to courts. The Prophet forbade insulting and abusing believers.[6] Muslim societies today differ: some criminalise, some tolerate quietly, and a minority of Muslim thinkers argue for a different reading.
