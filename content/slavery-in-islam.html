Slavery was a fact of the world into which the Quran came, as it was in Rome, Persia and Arabia. A fair account asks what the sources did to it, and what they did not do.

The Quran asks "What will make you know the steep path? It is the freeing of a slave."[1] Freeing a slave is the expiation for breaking an oath, for killing by mistake and for breaking the fast of Ramadan, and it is a use of zakāh funds.[2] No other institution in the Quran has so many exits written in.

It gives two options for captives of war: "either release as a favour or ransom."[3] Enslavement is not one of them. Classical jurists added it from the practice of the age, and many modern scholars hold that the Quran's two options bind.

"Those of your slaves who seek a contract of freedom, write it for them if you know good in them, and give them of God's wealth."[4] This is the mukātaba, and the owner is told to accept. The sources record that the Prophet encouraged it and the funds of zakah can be used to pay.

The Prophet said: "They are your brothers. God has put them under your hand. Let him who has his brother under his hand feed him from what he eats and clothe him from what he wears, and not burden him beyond his capacity."[5] A master who struck his slave was told to free him as expiation.[6] A woman who bore her master's child became an umm walad and could not be sold, and was free on his death.

Bilal was the first muezzin. Zayd ibn Haritha, a freed slave, commanded armies. The Mamluk dynasty ruled Egypt and Syria for over two and a half centuries, the Delhi Sultanate had a "slave dynasty", and several of the Caliph's generals were freed slaves.

Muslim scholars today hold slavery to be forbidden. The conditions that justified it, which were the exchange of captives in war, no longer exist. International law and the laws of every Muslim country prohibit it, and fatwas by scholars condemn the groups that revived it.
