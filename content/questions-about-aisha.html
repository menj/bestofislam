Aisha bint Abu Bakr is the wife of the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) about whom the most questions are asked, and most of the questions come from one report. This article takes five of them in turn, gives the sources, and says where the evidence is disputed. It should be read with the rest of her record, which is long.

The best-known report, in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim, is from Aisha herself: that she was married at six and the marriage was consummated at nine.[1] Most classical scholars accepted it, and it is the view stated in most books.

Some modern scholars question it, on the grounds that other reports point to an older age. One argument compares her age with that of her sister Asma, who by the biographical dictionaries was about ten years older and died at 100 in 73 AH. That would make Aisha about seventeen or eighteen at the time of the marriage.[2] Others point to reports that she took part in the battle of Uhud as a water carrier.[3] The report in Bukhari is stronger by chain; the arguments against it are about internal consistency of the dates. The question is open among serious scholars.

In seventh-century Arabia, as in most of the world before the modern age, marriages were arranged by families, often at young ages, and the sources give no sign that her contemporaries found it remarkable. Quraysh attacked the Prophet for many things, including calling him a poet and a madman, and none of the reports record an attack on this marriage.

This is a point about the historical record. It is not a case that the practice is right now, and Muslim scholars today do not argue that it is. Egypt, Tunisia and Turkey set a minimum marriage age of 18, and the governing Islamic principle is that no marriage is valid without maturity and consent.[4]

The record shows a woman with strong views and a place in public life. She argued with the Prophet and with the Companions, and he is reported to have laughed at her answers. When the Quran later gave the wives a choice between worldly life and staying with him, she was the first to be asked and chose him.[5]

She also corrected senior Companions. The scholar al-Zarkashi collected her corrections of other Companions in a book, al-Ijāba.[6]

She is credited with 2,210 hadith, one of the largest bodies from any Companion, and senior Companions came to her on matters of law, especially on the Prophet's private life, the rules of purity and the details of prayer, which only his family knew.[7]

After his death she taught men and women from her room, with a screen for those who needed it. Her pupil Urwa ibn al-Zubayr, and through him much of the school of Medina, preserved her opinions.

In 627, on the return from a raid, she was left behind by accident and brought back to the army by a Companion, Safwan. Some in Medina spread a rumour, led by Abdullah ibn Ubayy. The Prophet waited a month, without revelation, before the Quran spoke: "Those who brought the lie are a group among you."[8] Ten verses clear her and warn the community against slander.[9]

The same passage gave the law that a charge of adultery needs four witnesses, and a false charge is flogged.[10]
