Two verses draw the same challenge. The Quran says some Jews called Ezra the son of God. It says Jesus will be asked whether he told people to take him and his mother as gods. Critics ask for the Jews and the Christians who believed either.

One of these has a clear answer. The other is a real difficulty, and I will treat it as one.

The verse has God asking Jesus whether he told people to take him and his mother as two gods besides God, and Jesus denying it.[1] There is no mention of a Trinity in that verse, and no claim that Christians define God as Father, Son and Mary.

The Quran does address the Trinity, separately, when it rejects saying God is the third of three.[2] Merging the two verses into "Muslims think Mary is in the Trinity" creates a claim the text does not make.

Epiphanius of Salamis, writing around 376, describes women in Arabia who offered cakes to Mary as a goddess, and he rebukes them for worshipping her.[3] Some scholars doubt the sect existed as he describes it.

The point does not depend on that sect. The verse asks whether Mary was taken as a god. That is a question about worship, and the history of Marian devotion is long enough that a church father felt the need to warn against it.

The Quran reports that some Jews said Ezra is the son of God.[4] No surviving Jewish text says this in those words. That is the honest starting point, and a Muslim who pretends otherwise hands the critic an easy win.

Classical exegetes read the verse as describing a particular group within Judaism. The tradition names Sallam ibn Mishkam and his companions, Jews of Medina who disputed with the Prophet, among those who said it.[5]

The absence of a surviving Jewish text also proves less than it seems. As the historian F. E. Peters observed, the Quran is very nearly the only source for what Jews in seventh-century Arabia believed.[6] A belief held by one community in Medina could leave no trace in the rabbinic literature of Babylon and Palestine.

Ezra is remembered as the man who restored the Torah after the exile, and later Jewish literature raises him close to Moses. A text circulating in the early centuries has God tell him that he will be taken up from among men, to live with God's son.[7] Jewish writings of the same period called angels the children of God. "Son of God" was also a title Jewish scripture applied to Israel and its kings. An exaltation of Ezra expressed in that vocabulary, among one community, is not far-fetched.

It is also not proven. Honesty requires saying both.

The bottom line

On Mary, the objection attacks a claim the Quran does not make. On Ezra, the absence of a surviving text is real, and the answer rests on the verse's first audience and on Ezra's standing in Jewish memory.

One verse is answered. The other is argued. Know which is which before you debate either.
