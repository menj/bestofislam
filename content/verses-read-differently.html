Most Christian-Muslim argument comes down to a handful of verses. Each side reads them and sees something obvious. Here are 7, with both readings, and what the disagreement actually turns on.

Christians: a declaration of shared divine nature.[1]

Muslims: unity of will and purpose, the same sense in which Jesus prays a few chapters later that his disciples may be one as he and the Father are one.[2] Nobody reads that second verse as making the disciples divine, so the word "one" can't carry divinity by itself.

Christians: an echo of God's name from the burning bush, a claim to eternal existence.[3]

Muslims: pre-existence in God's foreknowledge and plan, a status the Quran also gives to messengers, and a phrase that the Greek grammar doesn't obviously tie to the divine name. The crowd's reaction, picking up stones, shows they took it as blasphemy. It doesn't show they were right.

Christians: the clearest confession of Jesus as God in the gospels.[4]

Muslims: an exclamation, spoken by a man who had just seen someone he thought dead, and unrepeated by any other disciple anywhere. If the confession were the doctrine, it's odd that it appears once, from the doubter, and is never taught.

Muslims: plain subordination.[5]

Christians: greater in role during the incarnation, equal in nature. The Muslim reply is that this reading imports a distinction the text doesn't make, and that every hard verse gets the same treatment: the divine nature says the exalted things, the human nature says the rest, and the doctrine is never at risk from any verse because the doctrine decides in advance which nature is speaking.

Muslims: Jesus refuses the compliment and redirects it to God alone.[6] A being who was God would have no reason to.

Christians: a question meant to make the young man think about what he was implying, not a denial. The Muslim response is that this is what the verse would have to mean for the doctrine to survive it, which is different from what it says.

Christians: the Logos is God, and became flesh.[7]

Muslims: the Quran also calls Jesus a word from God.[8] A word belongs to its speaker; it isn't the speaker. And the Greek of that clause has been argued over since antiquity, since the noun for God appears without the article, which is why some early readers took it as "the Word was divine" and stopped short of identifying the two.

Christians: Jesus as the sole way to salvation.[9]

Muslims: the sole way for his audience, in his time, as each prophet is for his people. The Quran has every messenger saying the equivalent to his own community. The verse is exclusive in the way a doctor's instruction is exclusive: binding on the patient in front of him.

The bottom line

Notice the shape. In every case, the Christian reading needs the doctrine of 2 natures, or the Trinity, to be in place before the verse is read, and the Muslim reading needs nothing added.

That's not proof the Muslim reading is right. Sometimes the simple reading is wrong. But it does put the burden where it belongs: on the side that has to bring something to the text to make it say what they need.
