A popular story holds that when Muslim armies took Alexandria in the 640s, the caliph ʿUmar ordered its great library destroyed, reasoning that books agreeing with the Quran were unnecessary and books disagreeing with it were harmful. The books, the story goes, heated the city's bathhouses for six months.

Historians who have examined the sources, Muslim and non-Muslim alike, have concluded that the story is a legend. Here are the five facts behind that conclusion.

The conquest of Alexandria took place in 641 or 642. The earliest surviving mention of the burning comes from ʿAbd al-Laṭīf al-Baghdādī, a physician from Baghdad who visited Egypt in 1203 and referred to it in a single passing phrase.[1]

The full story, with ʿUmar's reasoning and the six months of bathhouse fires, first appears in a history of learned men written by the Egyptian scholar Ibn al-Qifṭī around 1227. The later Christian historian Barhebraeus copied Ibn al-Qifṭī's account.[1]

The Muslim conquest of Egypt is recorded in a rich historical literature. None of it, for nearly six hundred years, notices the destruction of the most famous library in the ancient world.[1]

The historian Bernard Lewis, who studied the question closely, put the difficulty plainly: anyone who accepts the story must explain how so dramatic an event went unmentioned and unnoticed by every earlier writer.[1]

The library's decline began under its own rulers, who drove out many of its scholars in 145 BC, and continued under Rome.[2] Part of it burned in 48 BC, in the fire that spread from the harbour during Julius Caesar's war in the city.[2] Between 270 and 275 a Palmyrene invasion and the imperial counterattack probably destroyed whatever remained of the main library.[3] In 298 the emperor Diocletian set fire to the city after an eight-month siege.[4]

The library's daughter collection at the Serapeum, a temple of the god Serapis, lasted longer. The temple was demolished in 391 under a decree of Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria; whether it still held books by then is unknown.[3][5] By the time the Arabs arrived, two and a half centuries later, there was almost certainly no great library left to burn.

The tale ends with the library's books feeding the furnaces of Alexandria's bathhouses for six months. Ibn al-Qifṭī himself closed his account with a note of wonder, and admitted that he had been told the number of bathhouses and had forgotten it.[1]

The numbers do not survive scrutiny either. The count of bathhouses that later retellings supply comes from other Arabic sources, in quite different contexts.[1]

It is sometimes said that the story was invented to blacken Islam. Lewis rejected that explanation: the story's original sources are Muslim writers, and its one Christian source copied from a Muslim author. He thought it more likely that those writers adapted folklore already in circulation.[1][6]

Muslim scholarship was also among the first to take the story apart. In 1892 the Indian historian Shibli Nomani published a full examination of the charge against ʿUmar and concluded that the library had been lost centuries earlier.[7]

The bottom line

The Library of Alexandria declined over centuries, through fire, war and neglect, and whatever remained of it had almost certainly vanished long before the Arab conquest.

The story of ʿUmar's order appears six centuries after the event, in sources that cannot bear its weight. The honest verdict, reached by Muslim and Western historians alike, is that it is a legend.
