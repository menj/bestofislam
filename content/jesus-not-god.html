Start with what Muslims do believe about Jesus, because it surprises people. Born of a virgin. Performed miracles. Called the Messiah. Alive now, and returning. The Quran devotes more space to Mary than the New Testament does.

What Muslims do not believe is that he was God. Here is why, argued as far as possible from the Christian sources, since those are the ones the disagreement is about.

Jesus prays throughout the gospels. In the garden before his arrest he asks that the cup pass from him, and adds that it should be as the Father wills and not as he wills.[1]

Prayer is what a creature does towards its creator. A will that defers to another will is not the same will. Muslims read that scene as decisive: this is a man in distress, asking God for something, and submitting when the answer is no.

"The Father is greater than I."[2] The standard Christian reading is that he meant in his human nature only. That reading requires the doctrine of 2 natures to already be in place before the verse is read. Take the verse on its own and it says what it says.

Asked when the end would come, he said no one knows, not the angels, not the Son, only the Father.[3] A being that does not know something is not omniscient. Christian theology has an answer, again through the 2 natures. Muslims note that the answer is always the same answer, and that it was worked out centuries later to reconcile texts that read more simply without it.

The gospels contain no sentence in which Jesus says "I am God" or "worship me". The verses cited instead are read as implications: "I and the Father are one",[4] "before Abraham was, I am",[5] and the like. Each has an alternative reading that the earliest Jewish followers of Jesus would have recognised, unity of purpose, pre-existence in God's plan.

Muslims find it strange that the central claim of a religion would rest on inference when the man had every opportunity to say it plainly, and said so many other things plainly.

The Quran calls Jesus a word from God, a spirit from him, the Messiah, a messenger, a servant. It has him deny in the strongest terms that he told anyone to take him as a god.[6]

For a Muslim this settles it, since the Quran is revelation. For a non-Muslim it does not, and I do not pretend it does. That is why the first 4 reasons come from the other side's book.

The bottom line

A man who prayed, said the Father was greater, did not know the hour, and never claimed the title. Muslims read those 4 facts the simple way and say: a prophet, honoured beyond nearly any other, and a man.

Christians read them through a doctrine that took 4 centuries to settle. The question is which reading needs the least added to it.
