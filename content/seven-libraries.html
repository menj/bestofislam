A library is the easiest thing in a civilisation to lose and the hardest to rebuild. A shelf of books takes generations to gather, and a single season of war, famine or fashion can scatter it.

Here are seven libraries of the Muslim world, from Baghdad to Timbuktu, and the different ways they ended. Some fell to invaders and some were broken up by their own rulers or soldiers. One survives because a restoration was paid for, and one was kept whole across the centuries. Where the sources give only reported figures, the entry says so.

Founded under the Abbasids in the early 9th century, expanded under al-Ma'mūn into a translation institute, observatory and library. Greek, Persian and Indian works came in; Arabic versions went out, along with new work in mathematics, astronomy and medicine built on top of them. The mathematician al-Khwārizmī, whose treatise on al-jabr gave algebra its name, worked in the circle around it.

Historians differ on how formal the institution was. Some hold that "House of Wisdom" named a palace library of the caliphs, and that the large movement of translation was paid for by many patrons, including officials and scholars' families.[1] The point stands either way: Baghdad in this period paid scribes and translators in sums that made the work a profession.

It ended in 1258 when the Mongols took Baghdad. The story that the Tigris ran black with ink is probably embellished. The destruction was not. Very little of the collection survived in place, though a great deal of its content had already travelled.

The Umayyad caliph of Cordoba, who reigned from 961 to 976, was a collector on a scale nobody in Europe could match. Estimates of the 10th-century library run to 400,000 volumes, with agents buying in Cairo, Damascus and Baghdad and a catalogue that reportedly filled 44 volumes on its own. The figures come from later chroniclers and are probably generous, though the library was undoubtedly very large.

It did not survive its patron by long. His successor's chief minister, al-Manṣūr, had works on philosophy and astronomy pulled and burned to appease the religious scholars, a purge recorded by the Andalusian judge Ṣāʿid al-Andalusī.[2] The rest was dispersed in the civil wars that broke the caliphate after 1009.

The episode is worth stating plainly. The danger to this library came from inside the Muslim state, from a minister who needed the support of the jurists, and the loss of the books was a political price.

The Fatimid caliphs of Egypt built a collection that visiting scholars described in superlatives, with tens of thousands of volumes and dozens of copies of some titles. The Dār al-ʿIlm, meaning House of Knowledge, founded in 1005, opened it to the public. The Egyptian historian al-Maqrīzī, writing in the 15th century, preserves the best-known descriptions of its rooms and its holdings.[3]

It was looted in the 1060s during a famine and a mutiny, when unpaid soldiers sold books for whatever they could get. Bindings were reportedly used for shoe leather. What remained was dispersed when Saladin ended the dynasty in 1171.

Many of the numbers in the later accounts are doubtful. The sequence is not: a public library depended on the treasury, the treasury failed, and the soldiers who were owed pay took what they could sell.

A different kind of library: attached to a college. The Seljuk vizier Niẓām al-Mulk founded the Niẓāmiyya in 1065 as a teaching institution with an endowed collection to support it. Al-Ghazālī taught there.

It outlived the Mongol sack of 1258 in reduced form and continued into the 14th century before fading. Its model, an endowed college with a library as its heart, is the one that Oxford and Paris would follow.

That last claim is argued rather than settled. The historian George Makdisi held that the endowed college of medieval Europe owed its form to the Islamic madrasa, while other historians doubt that the link can be shown.[4] What is not in dispute is the design of the madrasa itself: the founder gave property in endowment, the income paid teachers and bought books, and the arrangement lasted as long as the property did.

The mosque and university that share its name were founded in Fez in 859, and the library grew with them and is still open. It holds a 9th-century Quran on gazelle parchment and a 14th-century copy of Ibn Khaldūn's introduction to history. Some accounts date the library proper to the Marinid sultan Abū ʿInān in the 14th century, which makes it younger than the mosque and still centuries old.

It nearly went the other way. By the 20th century the building was decaying and the manuscripts with it. A restoration completed in 2016 saved the collection. It is often called the oldest working library in the world, which is a title it holds because someone paid to keep it.

Timbuktu held hundreds of private family collections, built up from the 14th century, when Timbuktu was a trading and scholarly hub at the edge of the Sahara. Estimates of surviving manuscripts run into the hundreds of thousands, on law, astronomy, medicine, and commerce. The scholar Aḥmad Bābā, taken from the city to Morocco after the invasion of 1591, is said to have remarked that his own books, some 1,600 volumes, were the fewest among his friends.

They were nearly lost twice. Colonial disruption scattered them in the 19th century. In 2012 armed groups occupied the city, and when they left in January 2013 they set fire to the Aḥmad Bābā Institute. Reports put the loss at a few thousand manuscripts. Most of the collection had already been smuggled out by families and archivists, and it is now being catalogued and digitised.[5]

The Ottoman one, and the one that mostly worked. Built as part of the mosque complex in the 1550s and fed by endowments over the following centuries, it grew into the largest manuscript library in the Islamic world.

Its collection was never sacked. It absorbed other Ottoman collections as they closed, and today holds around 100,000 manuscripts, catalogued and largely digitised. If you want to know what a library looks like when each government in turn keeps paying for it, this is the example.

The bottom line

The seven ended in different ways. Baghdad fell to invaders, and Timbuktu was attacked in our own time and mostly rescued. Cordoba and Cairo were broken up by their own side, through a purge in one case and an unpaid army in the other. The Niẓāmiyya faded, the al-Qarawiyyīn was saved by a restoration, and the Süleymaniye was kept whole.

The pattern is plain: libraries survive exactly as long as someone is paying to keep them, and not a year longer. Conquest is the dramatic cause of loss, though the quieter ones, a purge, a missed payroll, a roof left unrepaired, account for as many.
