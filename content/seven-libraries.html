A library is the easiest thing in a civilisation to lose and the hardest to rebuild. Here are 7, and the different ways they ended.

Founded under the Abbasids in the early 9th century, expanded under al-Ma'mūn into a translation institute, observatory and library. Greek, Persian and Indian works came in; Arabic versions went out, along with new work in mathematics, astronomy and medicine built on top of them.

It ended in 1258 when the Mongols took Baghdad. The story that the Tigris ran black with ink is probably embellished. The destruction wasn't. Very little of the collection survived in place, though a great deal of its content had already travelled.

The Umayyad caliph of Cordoba was a collector on a scale nobody in Europe could match. Estimates of the 10th-century library run to 400,000 volumes, with agents buying in Cairo, Damascus and Baghdad and a catalogue that reportedly filled 44 volumes on its own.

It didn't survive its patron by long. His successor's chief minister, al-Manṣūr, had works on philosophy and astronomy pulled and burned to appease the religious scholars, and the rest was dispersed in the civil wars that broke the caliphate after 1009.

The Fatimid caliphs of Egypt built a collection that visiting scholars described in superlatives, with tens of thousands of volumes and dozens of copies of some titles. The Dār al-ʿIlm, meaning House of Knowledge, founded in 1005, opened it to the public.

It was looted in the 1060s during a famine and a mutiny, when unpaid soldiers sold books for whatever they could get. Bindings were reportedly used for shoe leather. What remained was dispersed when Saladin ended the dynasty in 1171.

A different kind of library: attached to a college. The Seljuk vizier Niẓām al-Mulk founded the Niẓāmiyya in 1065 as a teaching institution with an endowed collection to support it. Al-Ghazālī taught there.

It outlived the Mongol sack of 1258 in reduced form and continued into the 14th century before fading. Its model, an endowed college with a library as its heart, is the one that Oxford and Paris would follow.

Founded in the 9th century alongside the mosque and university that share its name, and still open. It holds a 9th-century Quran on gazelle parchment and a 14th-century copy of Ibn Khaldūn's introduction to history.

It nearly went the other way. By the 20th century the building was decaying and the manuscripts with it. A restoration completed in 2016 saved the collection. It is the oldest working library in the world, which is a title it holds because someone paid to keep it.

Not one library but hundreds: private family collections built up from the 14th century, when Timbuktu was a trading and scholarly hub at the edge of the Sahara. Estimates of surviving manuscripts run into the hundreds of thousands, on law, astronomy, medicine, and commerce.

They were nearly lost twice. Colonial disruption scattered them in the 19th century. In 2012 armed groups occupied the city and burned what they found in the main institute. Most of the collection had already been smuggled out by families and archivists, and it is now being catalogued and digitised.

The Ottoman one, and the one that mostly worked. Built as part of the mosque complex in the 1550s and fed by endowments over the following centuries, it grew into the largest manuscript library in the Islamic world.

It never burned and was never sacked. It absorbed other Ottoman collections as they closed, and today holds around 100,000 manuscripts, catalogued and largely digitised. If you want to know what a library looks like when the state that founded it doesn't collapse, this is the example.

The bottom line

Three were destroyed by conquest. Two by their own side, through purge or neglect. One was saved by a restoration, and one was never lost at all.

The pattern isn't that libraries burn. It's that libraries survive exactly as long as someone is paying to keep them, and not a year longer.
