The argument, as its proponents put it:

The Quran affirms the scriptures given to Moses and Jesus. It tells the People of the Book to judge by what God revealed in them, and it is addressing people alive in the 7th century. So either those scriptures were intact then, which makes our copies reliable and puts them in conflict with the Quran, or they were already corrupt, which means the Quran issued an order nobody could follow.

Two horns, no exit. Very tidy.

Tidiness is what should make you look closer. A dilemma only works if the third option is properly closed off, and this one needs 5 separate assumptions to close it.

The Quran talks about the injīl, the evangel, as something given to Jesus. Singular. A revelation he received.

Matthew, Mark, Luke and John are narratives about him, written by other people, in a language he did not preach in, and even mainstream critical scholarship dates the earliest of them decades after his death.

Whether the Quranic injīl refers to those 4 documents is the question the whole argument turns on. The argument does not answer it. It assumes it on the way past.

The argument runs a binary. Either a text is wholly reliable or it is worthless.

Nothing in the Quranic vocabulary of taḥrīf, meaning distortion, demands that. The Quran describes people concealing part of what they were given, writing things with their own hands and calling it revelation, and twisting the text with their tongues.

Every one of those describes a mixed state. Truth still in there, alteration alongside it.

A scripture in that condition is still worth consulting for what survives in it, which is what an instruction to judge by it would mean.

Classical exegetes split on this, and the split is old.

Some held the written text was altered. Others held the alteration was in the reading, with the words left standing and their sense inverted.

Take the second view and the dilemma just evaporates: the text stays consultable while its custodians read it wrong.

An argument advertised as inescapable has been answered for 1,000 years by a mainstream position within the tradition. That says something about how carefully the tradition was consulted before the argument was built.

The verse everyone quotes sits inside a passage that also calls the Quran muhaymin over what came before it. Guardian. Criterion. Quran 5:48.[1]

That is a claim of authority over the earlier books.

So the instruction to the People of the Book, telling them to judge by what they hold, comes from a text that in the same breath sets itself up as the standard their holdings get measured against. Quote the instruction, drop the clause 1 verse later, and you have manufactured the dilemma. Put the clause back and it has gone.

Those verses address particular communities in a particular dispute. Several of them concern a legal question the People of the Book brought to the Prophet for judgment, and the reproach is that they were holding a scripture whose ruling they would not apply.

Turning that into a timeless endorsement of every manuscript tradition that reached the 21st century is a long stretch. The argument makes the stretch without mentioning that it is making one.

The bottom line

For the dilemma to hold, all 5 have to hold. The injīl has to be the 4 gospels. Corruption has to be all or nothing. Distortion has to be textual only. The Quran has to defer to books it calls itself guardian over. And a situational instruction has to be a universal one.

Knock out any 1 of them and there is a third option sitting right there.

One thing I will say for the people pushing this argument: most of them know these responses exist. The real disagreement is narrower and more technical than the marketing suggests, and it is about which reading of taḥrīf the Quranic vocabulary will carry. That is a debate worth having. It is not a trap.
