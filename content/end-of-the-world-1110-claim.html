One widely circulated critique says that Muhammad (peace be upon him) predicted the end of the world 500 years after his call, which gives the year 1110 CE. The date passed, so the critique concludes that he was a false prophet.

The claim rests on reports about "half a day". Five points follow: what the report says, what the Quran says about the Hour, what the wording allows, how a day is measured, and how the Prophet and the scholars handled the question. Where a report's wording could not be confirmed, the article says so.

The claim in its strongest form runs as follows. Abu Dawud records a saying in which the Prophet hoped that his community would be given a delay of half a day before its Lord. The narrator, Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas, asked how long half a day was, and the answer given was 500 years.[1] Counting from the first revelation in about 610 CE gives 1110 CE, a date that passed long ago.

Part of this is sound. The report exists, it sits in a major collection, and the figure 500 is in the report itself. The critique's route through "a day is 1,000 years" is therefore a detour, because the report supplies the number directly. The critique also cites a passage of al-Tabari's History on the span of the world.[2] Its wording could not be checked for this article, so no claim is made about it. Whether this is the Prophet's most often repeated prophecy also cannot be confirmed here. The report above is the one the claim depends on, and the entries that follow examine it.

Several passages say that the time of the Hour belongs to God alone. Quran 7:187 says that knowledge of it is with the Lord, that none can reveal its time but Him, and that it comes suddenly. Quran 33:63 gives the same answer to people who ask, and adds that the Hour may well be near. Quran 31:34 lists it among the matters that only God knows.[3]

Quran 79:42-44 describes the questioners asking when it will arrive, and the reply is that its final term is with the Lord.[4] A critic can fairly point to 33:63 as a statement of nearness. It is a statement of nearness with no year attached, and the same verse tells the Prophet to say that the knowledge is with God. A date would contradict all of these verses, so a report that sounds like a date has to be read in a way that fits them.

As reported, the Prophet says "I hope" (arjū). He does not say that the world will end. What he hopes for is that his community will not fall short of being given half a day, which means that it will last at least that long.[5] A floor is not a deadline. The community has lasted far more than 500 years, so the hoped-for minimum was met and nothing in the report failed.

A critic may answer that the saying still implies that the end might come soon after the half day. Nothing in the wording states that. The report also gives no starting point. Counting from the call gives 1110 CE; counting from the migration to Medina in 622 CE gives 1122 CE; counting from his birth gives another year. The choice of 610 CE is the critique's own. Its grading as a hadith (a report of the Prophet's words or acts) should also be checked in a grading work before it is relied on either way, and this article does not assert one.

The critique's own arithmetic depends on Quran 22:47, which says that a day with the Lord is like a thousand years of human counting. Quran 32:5 uses a similar figure for the affairs that rise to Him. Quran 70:4 speaks of a day whose measure is fifty thousand years.[6] The Quran therefore uses more than one measure, and none of these verses is a conversion table for the Hour.

Half of 1,000 is 500, so a reader can link the report to 22:47, and some have. As far as could be confirmed, the exchange itself gives only a number and does not cite the verse; the link is an inference by readers. It is a reasonable inference, and also an optional one. The report holds up without it, since the number 500 is already there and the question is only what the number means.

In the well-known hadith of Gabriel, the Prophet was asked about the Hour and replied that the one asked knows no more than the one asking.[7] That is a direct statement of the limit of his own knowledge. Other reports have him joining two fingers and saying that he and the Hour were sent like these two, which speaks of closeness in the order of history and gives no date.[8]

The later scholars did not conclude that the passing of 500 years refuted him. Al-Suyuti, as I recall, wrote a short treatise arguing from the half-day report that the community would outlast a thousand years.[9] The exact title and argument should be verified before quotation. The wider point stands without it: the classical scholars read these reports as sayings about duration and the signs of the Hour, and none that this article could confirm treated 1110 CE as a fixed date.

The bottom line

The report behind the claim is real, and it does contain the number 500. It is a hope that the community would last at least half a day, it names no starting point, and the Quran and the hadith of Gabriel both place the time of the Hour with God alone. A minimum that is exceeded is not a failed prediction.

A critic is entitled to ask for the report's grading and for the passage in al-Tabari to be read in full. Those are fair requests, and they should be answered from the texts and not from summaries on either side.
