Quraysh, the tribe of Mecca, was proud of rank and lineage. The first Muslims included people with neither. The six below came from the bottom, from slavery, from another land, from poverty, and all went on to shape the community. Their stories are the early sources' answer to the idea that Islam was a movement of the powerful.

Bilal ibn Rabah was an Abyssinian slave of Umayya ibn Khalaf. When he became a Muslim his master took him out into the midday heat and laid a rock on his chest to make him renounce, and he answered "Ahad, Ahad" (One, One). Abu Bakr bought and freed him.[1]

The Prophet appointed him the first muʾadhdhin, the one who gives the call to prayer. After the conquest of Mecca he climbed the Kaaba to call.

Ammar ibn Yasir and his parents were tortured in Mecca. His mother Sumayya was killed by Abu Jahl and is counted the first martyr of Islam.[2] The Prophet passed them as they were being tortured and said, "Be patient, family of Yasir, for your promised place is Paradise."[3]

Ammar was forced to say words of disbelief under torture while his heart stayed firm, and the Quran's concession for such compulsion was revealed about the case.[4]

Salman al-Farisi is said to have left Persia searching for the truth, passed through Christian teachers, and ended in Medina, where he met the Prophet. He had been sold into slavery on the way. The Prophet helped him buy his freedom.[5]

He is credited with suggesting the trench at the battle of 627 to defend Medina. When the Companions argued over whether he was of the Ansar or the Muhajirun, the Prophet said, "Salman is one of us, the people of the house."[6]

Abu Dharr al-Ghifari came from a tribe known for highway robbery and became one of the earliest believers. He was known for austerity and blunt warnings to the wealthy about hoarding.[7]

The Quran's verse on those who "hoard gold and silver and do not spend it in the way of God" is linked to his remarks.[8] He died in exile in the desert at al-Rabadha in about 652.

Abu Hurayra was poor and spent his days with the Prophet, in the group called the people of the bench. He narrated more hadith than any other Companion, over five thousand.[9] He explained why: the emigrants were busy in the markets and the Ansar with their land, and he stayed to fill his stomach and to listen.[10]

His name means "father of the kitten".

Abdul Rahman ibn Awf emigrated to Medina without possessions. His new brother Sa'd ibn al-Rabi' offered him half his property and one of his wives. Abdul Rahman answered, "May God bless you in your family and wealth. Show me the market."[11]

He became one of the richest of the Companions, and spent his wealth on the cause, giving caravans of goods to the poor of Medina.
