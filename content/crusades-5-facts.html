The Crusades are retold on both sides as a clash of religions with a clear villain. The record is more tangled. These five facts are well documented, and each complicates the usual story.

In 1095 Pope Urban II preached at Clermont after an appeal from the Byzantine emperor Alexios I, whose empire had lost most of Anatolia to the Seljuk Turks after the battle of Manzikert in 1071. The Crusade was presented as help for the Eastern Christians and as a pilgrimage to Jerusalem.

The wider Muslim world was divided at the time. The Seljuk empire was breaking up into rival states and the Fatimid caliphate in Egypt was its enemy, so the invaders met no common front.

When the Crusaders took Jerusalem on 15 July 1099 they killed a large part of its Muslim and Jewish inhabitants. Their own chroniclers, such as Fulcher of Chartres and Raymond of Aguilers, describe streets running with blood, and Muslim writers such as Ibn al-Athir record the same.

The figures are disputed, but the fact is not. The city had been under Muslim rule since Umar accepted its surrender in 637 and gave its Christians a guarantee of their lives, churches and property.

Salah al-Din (Saladin) took Jerusalem in October 1187 after the battle of Hattin. The city was surrendered under terms. Residents could buy their freedom at a fixed ransom, and he released many who could not pay. The Jews were permitted to return, and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre was left in Christian hands.

The contrast is noted by historians of every background. The Latin Christians who could pay left, and the Orthodox and Eastern Christians remained.

In 1204 the Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople, the capital of the Christian Byzantine empire, and its soldiers looted churches and killed thousands. The Latin states set up in the East were often at odds with the local Orthodox, Armenian and Syriac Christians.

The Crusades were not a united front of Christendom against Islam. They were also a competition among Christian powers.

Medieval Muslims called the invaders al-Ifranj, "the Franks", and wrote little about "Crusades" as a religious war. Interest in the term grew in the nineteenth century, with European colonial writing and then with Arab nationalism.

Modern speakers on both sides use it as shorthand for a grievance or a banner, and the historical event is much smaller than the label.
