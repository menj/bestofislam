The objection goes like this. If Abraham and Ishmael built the Kaaba, why did the first Muslims pray towards Jerusalem? Turning their backs on God's own house makes no sense, unless the Mecca story came later.

The first thing to notice is where the critic learned that Muslims once faced Jerusalem.

The second chapter of the Quran records it openly. It anticipates that foolish people will ask what turned the believers from their former direction of prayer, and answers that east and west belong to God.[1] It then instructs the Prophet to turn his face towards the Sacred Mosque.[2]

A later invention does not usually announce, in scripture, the very fact that would expose it.

The same passage says the earlier qibla was appointed to test who would follow the Messenger and who would turn on their heels.[3] The first direction was a test of obedience, and the change was one too. Muslims face a direction because they are told to. The stone is not what they worship.

Facing Jerusalem put the first Muslims in the line of the prophets of Israel, whose sanctuary it was. The Quran's claim is continuity with that line, and then a return to the older house Abraham built.[4] Both directions sit inside the same story.

Muslim sources place the change around sixteen or seventeen months after the migration to Medina.[5] It was public, contested, and remembered: a mosque in Medina is still called the Mosque of the Two Qiblas. Contested, public events are the kind of history that is hard to rewrite.

Some revisionist writers argue that early mosques point towards Petra, so Mecca must be a later substitute. Specialists in the history of astronomy and mosque orientation have rejected this, pointing out that early Muslims had no precise way to compute the direction of Mecca over long distances and used a range of traditional methods. Imprecise early orientations are what you would expect, and they do not converge on Petra.

The bottom line

The Quran records the first qibla, names the objection it would draw, and gives the reason. An objection that is quoted, anticipated and answered in the text it is aimed at has already been heard.

Face Jerusalem, then face Mecca: in both cases, the one being obeyed is the one who gave the command.
