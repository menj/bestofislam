The crucifixion is the point where Islam and Christianity part most sharply. For Christians it is the centre of salvation; the Quran denies that his enemies killed or crucified him.

The debate is often argued as one question when it is three: whether the crucifixion happened, what the Quran means when it denies it, and whether one person's death can bear another's sins. Here are six facts, taking the three in turn.

The Roman historian Tacitus, writing around AD 116, records that Christ suffered the extreme penalty under Pontius Pilate in the reign of Tiberius.[1] Scholars broadly accept the passage as authentic and as an independent Roman witness.[1] Even historians who doubt much of the Gospels' account treat the execution as among its surest facts; one, John Dominic Crossan, called it as sure as anything historical can ever be.[2]

Muslims should state this plainly. The Quran's account runs against the consensus of historians, and Muslims hold to the Quran on the authority of revelation, which historical method by its nature cannot confirm or overturn.

The verse begins with a claim attributed to the Messiah's enemies: "Indeed, we have killed the Messiah, Jesus, the son of Mary, the messenger of Allah." It answers that "they did not kill him, nor did they crucify him", that it was made to appear so to them (shubbiha lahum, "it was made to resemble to them"), and that "Allah raised him to Himself".[3]

The denial is aimed at those who boasted of having killed him. The Quran's concern is that God's messenger was not defeated by his enemies, and that God, and no human court, decided his end.

Critics point out that the classical commentaries give incompatible accounts of who was made to resemble Jesus, from a loyal disciple to a volunteer. They are right. The scholar Todd Lawson, in a book-length study of the question, shows that these substitution stories fill the commentaries while the Quran itself names no one, and traces much of their material to sources outside Islam.[4]

The commentators themselves weighed the stories critically. Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī rejected several versions while holding to the orthodox position that God protected Jesus and raised him up.[5] Muslims are bound by the verse; the stories are attempts to explain it, and their disagreement is a disagreement among commentators.

The Quran does not make Jesus immortal. In the cradle he speaks of "the day I was born and the day I will die and the day I am raised alive".[6] God's promise to him is to "take you and raise you to Myself".[7]

What the Quran denies is that his enemies killed him. It leaves his death in God's hands, and tells his story as a rescue from those who sought to kill him.

Christians who hold that sin can be forgiven only through blood often cite a line in the Letter to the Hebrews. Yet that line itself says only that nearly everything is cleansed with blood.[8]

The law of Leviticus explains the exception. A worshipper too poor to bring two birds brings a tenth of an ephah of fine flour as a sin offering, "and he will be forgiven".[9] The prophet Ezekiel sets down the governing principle: the soul who sins shall die, and the son shall not bear the iniquity of the father.[10] The law of Moses says the same of punishment: every man shall be put to death for his own sin.[11]

Critics often point to the Day of Atonement as a model for one dying for many. On that day lots were cast for two goats. One was killed as the sin offering for the people. Over the other, the high priest confessed all of Israel's sins and laid them on its head; that goat was presented alive, carried their iniquities into the wilderness, and was released there.[12]

The one creature in the law on which a people's sins are laid is the goat that lives. The Quran states the principle the prophets taught: "no bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another".[13]

The bottom line

The three questions have three different answers. On the first, historians and the Quran disagree, and Muslims side with the Quran knowingly. On the second, the Quran denies that Jesus was killed by his enemies and leaves the manner of his rescue largely unexplained, which the commentators then tried to fill.

On the third, the Islamic objection to atonement by proxy stands whatever happened on the cross, because it rests on the Hebrew prophets' own teaching that each soul bears its own sin.
