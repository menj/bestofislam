The argument goes like this. A woman at Khaybar served the Prophet poisoned mutton. Muslim sources say he felt the effects of it for the rest of his life. So God did not protect him, so he was not a prophet, and Muslims convicted him from their own records.

Credit where it is due: this one uses Muslim material instead of inventing it. That is also where it comes apart, because the material says considerably more than the argument reports.

Khaybar happened in year 7 after the migration. He died in year 11.

In between he negotiated at Hudaybiyyah, took Mecca without a fight, campaigned at Hunayn and Tabuk, and delivered the farewell sermon to tens of thousands of people.

If the poison killed him, it took 4 years to do it while he ran a military and political campaign. Poison does not behave that way, and the sources do not say it did.

Bishr ibn al-Barāʾ ate from the same dish. He died shortly after, and the sources describe his symptoms progressing.

Same meal. Same poison. One man dead, the other alive for another 4 years.

Anyone using this episode to argue that God failed to protect his prophet has to explain the companion who received no protection whatsoever and died exactly as you would expect.

Asked why she had done it, the woman said she wanted to find out what he was. A king, and she would be rid of him. A prophet, and she assumed he would be told.

That is her framing, from the narrations, not something an apologist bolted on later.

The same narrations say he stopped the meal and told everyone to take their hands off the food because the foreleg had informed him it was poisoned. Believe that or do not. But the polemic needs the narration where he eats and needs to bin the narration where he is warned, and both come from the same collections through the same chains.

Picking one and dumping the other requires a reason. So far the only reason on offer is that one produces the desired conclusion.

The narration everyone cites has ʿĀʾishah reporting that in his final illness he said he still felt what he had eaten at Khaybar, and that he felt at that moment as though his aorta were being cut.

Read it again. That is a man describing pain, using a simile.

Some exegetes concluded the poison contributed to his death. Others held the narration records sensation and nothing more. Muslim scholars have argued about it openly for centuries, which tells you the sources are not hiding anything. You cannot suppress a text you have been publicly disputing since the 9th century.

Take the strongest version of the claim: the poison contributed.

The polemicist assumes that is damaging. Inside the tradition it is the opposite, because a man who dies from such an injury holds the rank of martyr, and several narrations frame his death in precisely those terms.

Worth remembering who else the objection catches. The Hebrew prophets were killed in numbers, and scripture itself laments it. Jesus, on the Christian account, was tortured and executed by the state.

A theology that treats violent death as disqualifying has a much bigger problem than Muhammad.

Where does the expectation of immunity even come from? Usually Mark 16:18, the promise that believers will pick up snakes and drink deadly poison unharmed.[1]

That verse sits in the longer ending of Mark, verses 9 to 20. It is missing from the earliest and best Greek manuscripts, and most modern critical editions and printed Bibles flag it as doubtful in a footnote or set it apart on the page.

The test the Hebrew Bible actually gives for a false prophet is different: his prophecy fails to come true, or he calls people to other gods.[2] Poison does not appear in it anywhere.

The bottom line

Start with the thing nobody comments on. This episode is in the Muslim sources because Muslims put it there. A tradition inventing an invincible founder had every chance to leave an assassination attempt out, and did not.

What the sources give you is a man who was warned, who stopped the meal, whose companion died where he lived, who went on for 4 more years, and who let the woman go.

The polemic keeps the first clause and drops the rest.

  1. Mark 16:18, in the longer ending, verses 9 to 20.
  2. Deuteronomy 18:20 to 22, cf. 13:1 to 5.
