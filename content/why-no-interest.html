Interest is the one commercial practice the Quran threatens with war. "If you do not give it up, then be informed of a war from God and His Messenger."[1] That language sets ribā apart from every other prohibition in the Quran on trade. Here are five reasons the sources and scholars give.

"God has permitted trade and forbidden ribā."[2] The verse separates the two on purpose. The Quran also records that those who defended interest said "trade is just like riba," and answers them with a single clause: God has made one lawful and the other not.

The Prophet's words go with it. He cursed the one who takes interest, the one who pays it, the one who records it and the two witnesses, and said they are the same in sin.[3]

The Quran's idea of lending is the qarḍ ḥasan, the good loan: "Who will lend to God a good loan, that He may multiply it for him many times?"[4] And for a debtor in difficulty: "If he is in hardship, then let there be postponement until a time of ease. But that you remit it as charity is better for you."[5]

Seen this way, a loan is help given in a hard hour, and charging for it turns the helper into the creditor of the helped.

A principle of Islamic commercial law says that gain is the counterpart of liability. The Prophet ruled in a case that "the profit goes with the responsibility."[6] A lender who takes a fixed return whatever happens to the borrower's business has the gain without the risk.

That is the heart of the prohibition. The lender who wants a share must share in the loss, which is what partnership is.

The Quran addresses the case that people most fear: "Do not consume ribā, doubled and redoubled."[7] A loan that grows on its own turns a hard month into a lasting burden, and the history of debt, from ancient peasants to modern payday borrowers, is a history of this growth.

The aim of the ban is to keep a person's need from becoming another's income. Its first concern is the borrower.

The Quran does not forbid profit. It points to trade and partnership. Islamic finance rests on contracts such as muḍāraba, in which one party gives capital and the other works, and the two share profit by agreed ratio and the capital owner bears loss; and on sale contracts with a disclosed profit margin.[8]

Most scholars hold that fixed bank interest is riba, a view stated by the Islamic Fiqh Academy in the 1980s. Some modern scholars dispute the application to modern banking, but they are a minority.
