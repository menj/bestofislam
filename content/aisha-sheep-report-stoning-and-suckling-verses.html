One report about a sheep and a sheet of paper has been used for generations to argue that the Quran lost verses. It appears in a canonical collection, and it deserves a full account of its chain and its readers.

The five points below take the report, the way it was transmitted, the criticism of its narrator, the disagreement over its grade and the replies of Ibn Hazm and al-Baqillani.

Critics quote a report in Sunan Ibn Majah. A'isha said that the verse of stoning and of suckling an adult ten times was revealed, that it was written on a paper kept under her pillow, and that a tame sheep came in and ate it after the Prophet died.[1] The conclusion drawn is that verses of the Quran were lost, and that the claim of a preserved book fails.

The same A'isha is also reported in Sahih Muslim as saying that the Quran once stated ten known sucklings make marriage unlawful, that this was replaced by five known sucklings, and that the earlier wording was still being recited when the Prophet died.[2] Her own words describe a ruling that was changed, which is a different thing from a chapter that disappeared.

Every version of the report traces back to A'isha through Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr ibn Hazm and Amra bint Abd al-Rahman. Yahya ibn Sa'id al-Ansari, whose version is in Sahih Muslim, and Malik, in the Muwatta', both leave out the sheep. The version of al-Qasim ibn Muhammad, narrated by al-Tahawi, leaves it out as well. Only Muhammad ibn Ishaq includes it, and his version is the one in Ahmad's Musnad and Ibn Majah.[3]

A story that three other narrators from the same source do not mention, and that appears in a single line of transmission, is an exception in the record, which is how the hadith scholars treated it.

The sources record criticism of Ibn Ishaq as a narrator of legal and doctrinal reports. Ahmad ibn Hanbal said that his narration cannot be used as proof, and would not accept him as the sole narrator of a report, since he merged the words of several narrators into one. Yahya ibn Ma'in called him weak in one report, al-Nasa'i said that he is not strong, and al-Daraqutni said that he is not an authority although his reports may be considered together with others.[4]

The editors of Ahmad's Musnad state that the chain is weak because Ibn Ishaq alone narrated it, and Ibn Qutayba held that Malik is more accurate than Ibn Ishaq.[5] The principle applied is that an odd report from a weaker narrator, which conflicts with the more numerous and more reliable narrators, is set aside.

Scholars did not all reach the same grading. The page of Ibn Majah 1944 on sunnah.com shows Darussalam's grade of hasan, and the fatwa site Islamweb reports that al-Albani graded the report hasan.[1][6] The editors of the Musnad call it weak, and the arguments of the previous point lead several scholars to the same view.[5]

A fair reading states the disagreement. Where the grading is hasan, the argument in the next point answers the objection, and where the grading is weak, the objection never begins.

Ibn Hazm took the report as established and still rejected the conclusion. The verses, he argued, were abrogated, so there was no need to keep them. The Companions had also memorised them, and if they had still been Quran, the loss of one sheet of paper would not have stopped them writing the verses into the compiled copy from memory.[7]

Al-Baqillani argued from the method of the transmission. The Prophet and the Companions memorised and wrote down the Quran, and it is implausible that a verse of the Book survived only on a single sheet under A'isha's bed.[8] The Quran's text rested on the community. The stoning ruling rests on the practice of the Prophet and his successors, as the previous article sets out.[9]
