Both sides claim their scripture was reliably transmitted. Both can point to evidence. The evidence is different in kind, and the difference is the whole argument.

The Quran was memorised in full by many of the Prophet's companions during his lifetime and recited publicly, daily, in prayer. The written copies were a check on the memory, not the other way round. That chain of memorisers has never broken; there are millions of people alive today who hold the whole text by heart.

The New Testament books were written, copied, and copied again. There was no parallel institution of memorisation. Reliability rests on the manuscripts alone.

Within 20 years of the Prophet's death the caliph Uthman had a standard written copy produced and distributed. Regional readings differ in pronunciation and a small number of word forms, all catalogued, all within a known tradition.

The New Testament has no equivalent moment. The text printed today is a scholarly reconstruction from thousands of manuscripts that disagree in hundreds of thousands of places. Most variants are trivial. Some are not, and the field of textual criticism exists to adjudicate them.

Quran manuscripts on parchment exist from the first century of Islam. The Birmingham leaves, radiocarbon dated to a range that overlaps the Prophet's lifetime, carry text matching the standard copy.

For the New Testament the earliest substantial manuscripts date from the 3rd and 4th centuries, 150 to 300 years after the events. Fragments are older; whole books are not.

Some passages printed in the New Testament are absent from the oldest manuscripts and were added later. The longer ending of Mark, the woman taken in adultery, the Trinitarian clause in the first letter of John: modern critical editions bracket or footnote all of them.

There is no comparable category for the Quran. No passage in the standard text is regarded, by any tradition, as a later insertion.

Muslims claim the Quran is the words God revealed, preserved letter by letter. It is a strong claim and it has to be defended at that strength.

Christians, in the main, do not claim that for the gospels. They claim inspiration of the authors and a text that is reliable enough. That is a weaker claim, easier to defend, and it concedes at the outset what the Muslim argument is trying to establish: that the text we have is not exactly what was written.

The bottom line

Memorisation alongside writing. A standard copy within a generation. Manuscripts from the first century. No recognised interpolations. A claim of exact preservation that the community is willing to be held to.

None of that makes the Quran true. It makes the question of what the Quran says a settled one, which is the question you have to settle before you can ask whether it is true.
