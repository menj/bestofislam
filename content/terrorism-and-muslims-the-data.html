Critics of Islam often say that terrorism is Islamic at its root, that most terrorism is committed by Muslims, and that the figures prove it. Part of this rests on real events, and the attacks of 11 September 2001 remain the starting point of the debate. Other parts rest on numbers that come with no source.

Below are five points: the figures as quoted, what the main databases record, what attackers cite and how scholars answer, how scholars have condemned the attacks, and three further claims about leadership, a sermon and weapons. Each claim is stated at full strength and then checked against sources. Where a figure could not be confirmed, the text says so.

One widely circulated critique says that 85 percent of terrorism is Islamic, that 4 percent of the world's population emulates Muhammad (peace be upon him), and that such terrorists are twenty times more lethal than others. Each number can be tested.

The population figure is the easiest. The Pew Research Center estimated that Muslims numbered about 1.8 billion in 2015, roughly a quarter of the world's people.[1] The critique's 4 percent is far from that count of Muslims, though it speaks of people who emulate the Prophet, a different and unmeasured group, so the two cannot be compared directly. The 85 percent and "twenty times" figures arrive with no named dataset, and no match to them was found in the Global Terrorism Database, the open record of attacks kept since 1970 by the START consortium at the University of Maryland.[2] The share of attacks and deaths linked to jihadist groups swings with the years, the countries and the definition of terrorism chosen. A single figure for all times and places needs a source and a stated method.

A fair reader will ask whether the true numbers are lower but still large. The next entry takes that up.

Jihadist groups have caused a very large number of deaths in recent decades. The Global Terrorism Index, published each year by the Institute for Economics and Peace from Global Terrorism Database records, has named the Islamic State, Boko Haram, the Taliban and al-Qaeda among the deadliest groups in its editions of the 2010s and early 2020s; check a given year before quoting a ranking.[3] The attacks of 11 September 2001 killed nearly 3,000 people in one morning.[4]

Other findings qualify the picture. The same index places most deaths in countries such as Iraq, Afghanistan, Pakistan, Syria and Nigeria, so most victims are local, and many are Muslims.[3] Europol's annual TE-SAT reports on the European Union counted more separatist than jihadist attacks in several years, although jihadist attacks caused most of the deaths.[5] A 2017 report of the US Government Accountability Office found that far-right extremists were behind more of the fatal extremist incidents in the United States after 11 September 2001 than jihadists were (the report did not count the 2001 attacks themselves).[6]

The defensible statement is narrower than the critique's: jihadist groups have been a leading cause of terrorism deaths worldwide in the past two decades, and most of their victims are Muslims.

Jihadist groups do quote scripture, and it would be a mistake to deny it. Al-Qaeda's 1998 statement leaned on Quranic passages about fighting. Groups commonly cite Quran 9:5, often called the sword verse, and a hadith (a report of the Prophet's words and deeds) in which he says he was ordered to fight people until they testify to God's oneness.[7] The critique concludes that the attackers follow Muhammad's orders.

Classical scholars read such texts as rules for war, held inside strict limits. The verses around 9:5 exempt those with whom treaties stand (9:4) and require that anyone seeking protection be escorted to safety (9:6). The Quran says that killing one person unjustly is like killing all of humankind (5:32) and forbids transgression even in fighting (2:190).[8] The Prophet forbade the killing of women and children in war,[9] and said that whoever kills a person under a covenant of protection will not smell the fragrance of Paradise.[10] Abu Bakr, the first caliph, gave his army the same instruction.[11] For these rules in detail, see War, killing and execution in Islam: 5 facts.

Hijacked airliners and office workers fall outside every one of these limits. Researchers disagree on what drives such attacks; some stress ideology, others occupation or social networks.[12]

Condemnation came from major institutions. After the attacks of 2001, Muhammad Sayyid Tantawi, then Grand Shaykh of al-Azhar, publicly condemned the killing of civilians.[13] In 2014 more than a hundred Muslim scholars from many countries signed an open letter to the leader of the Islamic State, arguing point by point that its killings, enslavement and declaration of a caliphate violate Islamic law.[14] In 2010 the Pakistani scholar Tahir ul-Qadri issued a book-length fatwa (a legal opinion) declaring terrorism and suicide bombing forbidden.[15]

Ordinary Muslims were asked as well. Pew surveyed Muslims in dozens of countries in 2011 and 2012 and published the results in 2013. In most countries, large majorities said that suicide bombing and other violence against civilians in defence of Islam is never justified, although support was higher in a few places.[16] The country figures need checking before they are quoted.

Scholars are not unanimous on every question. Some defended armed resistance against occupying forces, a separate dispute from the targeting of civilians, and Muslim jurists still argue about it. On the killing of civilians in New York, Washington and London, the leading voices in the tradition agree.

The critique says all Muslims must unite under a single leader, citing a hadith in Sahih Muslim (Book of Government): if allegiance is pledged to two caliphs, kill the second.[17] The report exists. Scholars have read it as a ruling against splitting a community that already has a lawful ruler, a matter of civil order inside a caliphate. No recognised caliph has existed since the Ottoman caliphate was abolished in 1924, and the 2014 open letter cited above argued that a caliphate cannot be declared by one armed group without the agreement of Muslims at large.

The critique also quotes a Saudi preacher on television calling for the enslavement of women. The sermon could not be verified here. A single preacher is not a source of Islamic law, and Saudi Arabia formally abolished slavery in 1962.[18]

Last, the critique forecasts that oil money will fund weapons of mass destruction and that a quarter of humanity will die within twenty-five years. No source is given, and no dataset could support such a forecast. Governments of every faith share the real concern about terrorists obtaining such weapons.

The bottom line

The sources support a sober statement. Jihadist groups have killed many thousands of people, most of them Muslims, and scripture is cited by those who kill. The figures of 85 percent, 4 percent and twenty times have no named source, and the texts the attackers cite carry limits that the tradition applied from its earliest years.
