Replies on X put the objection sharply: a perfectly preserved Quran is still corrupt, and a text copied faithfully is still false if its content is false. Others say that Muslims confuse the goodness of a message with the protection of its messenger's copyright. As a point of logic, the objection has force, and this article takes it seriously.

Below are five answers: where the objection is right, what preservation does establish, where Muslims make the case for truth, how the charge of errors in the content is answered, and how the same test applies to every scripture. Each answer also says where its own argument stops.

A text can be copied without a single letter changed and still be false. A forged letter reproduced by a careful scribe remains a forgery, and a mistaken textbook remains mistaken in every printing. Preservation is a claim about continuity: the words read today are the words first recited. Truth is a claim about the world, and the first claim does not entail the second.

Muslim scholars have long accepted this separation in practice. No classical author argued that the Quran is true because it was transmitted well. The sciences of transmission, such as the study of the recognised readings (qirāʾāt) and the study of hadith (reports of the Prophet's words and deeds) and their chains, were built to answer a historical question: what was said, and who said it?[1] Whether the content is correct is a different question, and the tradition handed it to theology and argument. A reader who hears "preserved, therefore true" as a complete proof has been given a weak argument, and the critic is right to decline it.

It establishes that the book we read is the book early Muslims read. That is a modest claim with real consequences. Critics and believers argue about the same words, the Quran can be judged on its own content instead of on a reconstruction, and later disputes about what the Prophet taught can be checked against a fixed text. Scholars differ on how close the earliest manuscripts come to his lifetime. The Birmingham leaves are the usual example, and radiocarbon testing of the parchment gave a range of roughly 568 to 645; some scholars point out that such a test dates the animal and not the writing.[2]

For Muslims it also settles a further matter. The Quran says that God sent down the reminder and will guard it (15:9).[3] If the text had been altered, the claim of the faith to hold God's unaltered word would fall with it. Muslims therefore defend preservation firmly, and the defence belongs to the faith itself: it is a premise. It is necessary for the Muslim case and insufficient as the whole case. The articles on manuscript transmission and on the copies attributed to Uthman set out the evidence and the points on which scholars differ.

Muslims who argue for truth usually offer three things. The first is the content of the Quran and its challenge to produce a chapter like it (2:23; 10:38; 17:88).[4] The second is the character of Muhammad (peace be upon him), whom the Quran describes as being of great moral stature (68:4) and whom his Meccan neighbours are reported to have called al-Amin, the trustworthy, before revelation, together with the community and civilisation that followed him.[5] The third is rational argument for God, such as the argument from the beginning of the universe developed by al-Ghazali.[6]

None of these is a proof in the way a calculation is a proof. Critics reply that literary excellence is a matter of taste and does not show divine origin, that sincere founders of many movements have won the trust of their followers, and that an argument for a creator reaches at most a creator and not this particular book. Muslims answer each reply, and the exchange continues. These are arguments that thoughtful people weigh differently, and the fair statement is that they are offered as a cumulative case. Readers may find the article on why the universe points to a creator a useful next step.

The charge that the preserved text contains errors deserves a direct reply. The first claim concerns Haman, named beside Pharaoh in 28:6, 28:38 and 40:36, whom critics place in the Persian court of the Book of Esther.[7] Defenders reply that a shared name across centuries proves nothing by itself, and that an Egyptian name and title have been proposed from inscriptions. The article on the Haman anachronism gives the detail.

The second claim concerns 18:86, where Dhu al-Qarnayn is said to find the sun setting in a murky spring. Many commentators read the verse as describing how the sun appeared to a traveller standing at the western edge of the land, and the word for murky has a recorded variant reading.[8] The third group is the science statements. The article on science claims graded by strength separates those that hold up from those that are weak, and Muslims themselves disagree about several. Some questions stay open, including the best reading of certain verses; the article on verses read differently shows how much depends on interpretation.

Christian and Jewish texts are not proven true by their preservation either, and Muslims who argue otherwise for the Quran alone apply a rule they would reject elsewhere. What manuscripts can show differs between the books. The earliest fragment of a Gospel, a small piece of John, is usually dated to the first half of the second century, and the great complete codices come from the fourth.[9] Christian scholars openly record many variant readings, nearly all of them minor. The Dead Sea Scrolls carry portions of the Hebrew Bible back by roughly a thousand years before the medieval codices.[10]

The Quran has surviving manuscripts from the first Islamic century, and a shorter interval between revelation and the earliest copies, which is a historical advantage. It also has recognised variant readings, and Muslims do not claim otherwise. The communities also claim different things: most Christians do not hold that the Gospels were dictated word for word, so for them preservation carries less weight. In each case manuscript evidence answers the question of what the text said. It leaves the question of whether the text is true to other arguments.

The bottom line

Preservation shows that the Quran we read is the Quran early Muslims recited. It does not show that the Quran is true, and the objection is correct to say so. For Muslims it supports one premise of the faith, that the text is the unaltered word of God, and the case for the rest rests on the content, the character of the Prophet and arguments for God.

Those arguments can be weighed and contested, as can the specific charges of error. A reader is entitled to examine them one at a time and to apply the same standard to every scripture.
