Walk into a mosque and you will see no pictures of God, and walls covered in pattern that seems to go on past its edges. The common explanation is that Islam "forbids images", and that the pattern is what artists did instead. The scholar Ismail al-Faruqi argued that this has the order wrong. The pattern is not a substitute. It is a positive statement of belief, worked out in line and colour.[1] Here are five ideas from his account.

The first article of Islamic faith is that there is no god but God, and the Quran adds that "nothing is like Him."[2] An image works by likeness, and so no image can be of Him. Faruqi's point is that the Muslim artist took this seriously. Greek and Roman art made its gods by perfecting nature, so that the god was nature at its best. Islam holds that God is wholly other than nature, so nature cannot be used to show Him.[3]

When the Muslim artist draws a vine, a leaf or a flower, he does not copy it and does not perfect it. He simplifies it until it is a shape. Faruqi called this the artist's way of saying "no" to nature: nothing in it is a fit vehicle for the divine, neither the actual flower nor its ideal form.[3] The same treatment is given to the rare human and animal figures in Islamic art, as in manuscript painting, which are drawn flat and emptied of the drama that the Western tradition prizes.

Stylisation, followed to its end, reaches the geometrical figure: the circle, the star, the polygon. These are the opposite of nature as the senses give it. A design built on them makes no reference to anything in the world, which suits the statement that nothing in the world is God.[3] This is why so much Islamic decoration is geometric, from tile to carpet to carved wood.

The Muslim designer developed a pattern in which each figure leads into the next. Repeated and symmetrical, it has no centre and no end, and it is cut off at the edge of the object, so that the eye keeps moving past it. Faruqi argued that the viewer's imagination tries to carry the pattern on, and fails, because it cannot be done. The failure is the point: it gives a sense of the infinite, and the infinite is what transcendence means.[3]

This, he said, is why calling the arabesque mere "decoration" misses it. The earliest examples he cites, from the Umayyad palaces of Jordan, the Dome of the Rock and the Great Mosque of Damascus, date from the first Islamic century, and the craftsmen were still often Christian or converts working in the Byzantine tradition.[3]

Faruqi traced the design to the Quran itself. Its text has no beginning, middle and end in the usual sense: a reader may open it anywhere and the recitation is complete in itself. Its rhythm carries the listener on, and the wish to go on is built in. The arabesque does with line what the Quran does with sound.[4] A Muslim who reads the Quran and looks at a mosque ceiling is meeting the same idea twice. Calligraphy, in which the words of the Quran become the design, joins the two.
