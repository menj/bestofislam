"Islamophobia" is used by governments, by rights groups and by Muslims, and it is also contested. Here are five facts about the word and the thing it names.

The term came into wide English use with a 1997 report by the Runnymede Trust in Britain, titled Islamophobia: a challenge for us all. It described unfounded hostility towards Islam and the fear and dislike of Muslims that follows. Hostility to Muslims, in Europe and elsewhere, is much older than the word.

Muslims have argued with Christians, Jews and one another for fourteen centuries, and they welcome an honest argument about their religion. The line the term draws is between a view and a person. Criticising a belief is part of a free discussion. Treating every Muslim as a threat, or denying their rights because of their faith, is another thing.

On 15 March 2019 a gunman killed 51 people at two mosques in Christchurch, New Zealand. Mosques, schools and individuals have been attacked in many countries, and Muslim women who dress visibly have been targets of abuse. In 2022 the United Nations General Assembly proclaimed 15 March the International Day to Combat Islamophobia.

The Global Terrorism Index has found that most deaths from terrorism in recent decades have occurred in Muslim-majority countries, among them Iraq, Afghanistan, Nigeria and Pakistan. Muslim scholars have condemned the attacks, among them the 2004 Amman Message and an open letter in 2014 from 126 scholars to the leader of ISIS. The picture of Muslims as the perpetrators leaves out who the victims are.

Some writers say that the term is used to shield ideas from criticism, and that it should be reserved for hatred of persons. Others say that dropping the word would hide a real and growing harm. Both positions can be held by people who agree that anti-Muslim violence is wrong.
