Critics of Islam often press one question hard. The hadith were written down after the Prophet's death, so why should anyone trust them, when Muslims question gospels written decades after Jesus?

It deserves a straight answer. Part of the answer is found in an unexpected place: the introduction to the Second Letter of Peter in the Bible that the United States Conference of Catholic Bishops publishes online.[1]

The bishops' introduction states that among modern scholars there is wide agreement that 2 Peter is pseudonymous: a work composed by a later author who attributed it to Peter, following a literary convention of the period.[1]

This comes from the Catholic Church's own scholarship, printed as the introduction to the book itself. A Muslim who said the same would be accused of hostility.

The letter opens in the name of Simon Peter, apostle of Jesus Christ.[2] It calls itself his second letter.[3] Its author says he was an eyewitness of the transfiguration and heard the voice from heaven.[4]

A later writer claiming to have stood on the mountain with Jesus is making a claim about himself that the evidence, on the bishops' own account, does not support.

According to the same introduction, many scholars consider 2 Peter the latest work in the New Testament, and date it to the first or second quarter of the second century.[1]

The letter gives its own reasons for that dating. It speaks of the apostles as a generation already gone.[5] It refers to Paul's letters as a known collection, some of it hard to understand, and ranks them with the other scriptures.[6] A collected Paul, already read as scripture and already disputed, belongs to a later church than Peter's.

The introduction notes that the passage on false teachers has many literary contacts with the Letter of Jude, and that 2 Peter is generally agreed to depend on Jude.[1] An apostle writing from his own memory of Jesus would have little need to rework another author's text.

The oldest certain reference to 2 Peter comes from Origen in the early third century, and Origen reports that others rejected it. Some local churches still excluded it from the canon as late as the fifth century.[1]

The early church doubted this letter for the same reason modern scholars do. It entered the canon in spite of that doubt, and it remains there.

The bottom line

The question for Muslims was why late writings should be trusted. The fairer question is what a writing carries with it.

A hadith comes with a named chain of transmitters, which Muslim scholars examined man by man and graded, rejecting a great many reports along the way. 2 Peter carries a famous name at its head, and the Catholic Church's own Bible says that name is probably not its author's.

Anyone who presses the charge of late and unreliable sources should apply it evenly. Applied evenly, it lands first on a letter that is canonical scripture in every major church.
