The headscarf is the most visible and most argued-over symbol of Muslim life. The argument is often about politics. The sources are about modesty, and they speak to both sexes.

The verse on dress opens with men: "Tell the believing men to lower their gaze and guard their private parts; that is purer for them."[1] Only afterwards does it address women. Modesty in the Quran starts with how a man looks and how he behaves, and the Prophet said that modesty is part of faith.[2]

"Tell the believing women to lower their gaze and guard their private parts and not display their adornment except what is apparent of it, and let them draw their khimār over their chests."[3] Another verse tells them to draw their outer garments close so that they are recognised and not harassed.[4] The khimār was a head-cover already worn in Arabia, and the verse tells women to draw it over the chest.

The classical schools agreed that a woman covers her hair and body in public. They differed on the face and hands: some held them uncovered, others covered. The disagreement is old and is still alive. Ismail al-Faruqi's glossary defines ḥijāb simply as the covering of a Muslim woman, the Islamic style of dress for women, and asks English writers to keep the Arabic word and explain it, not to render it "veil".[5]

"There is no compulsion in religion."[6] Many scholars cite the verse against forcing women to dress a certain way, and the practice of some states of enforcing the hijab by police has been criticised by Muslim scholars. The act has value, they say, when it is chosen. A rule imposed by force produces appearance without piety.

Banning the headscarf in a school or workplace is also a compulsion. Muslim women in Europe and elsewhere who wear it say they do so as an expression of faith and identity. Women who do not wear it are Muslims too, and they should not be pressed or insulted for it.
