A student who solves an equation or looks up at the night sky is using Arabic without knowing it. The words arrived in Europe with the books that carried them, mostly through translations made in Spain in the 12th and 13th centuries.

Here are 6 of them, and the route each one took.

Al-jabr means the restoring, or the setting of what is broken. It named one of the two operations in a 9th-century treatise by the mathematician al-Khwārizmī in Baghdad, a book that taught how to balance and solve equations.[1]

When the treatise was translated into Latin, the operation gave its name to the whole discipline. A surgeon who set bones was once called an algebrist in Spanish for the same reason.

This one is a man's name. Al-Khwārizmī's work on Indian numerals reached Europe in Latin as the work of Algoritmi, and the name came to stand for the method he taught: a fixed sequence of steps that produces a result.

Search engines and sorting routines run on algorithms, and each use of the word names a mathematician from Khwarazm.

The point directly overhead was called in Arabic samt ar-raʾs, the way over the head. A medieval Latin scribe misread the letter m in samt as ni, and the error was copied until it became the word.[2]

Zenith is, in the strictest sense, a mistake. It has named the top of the sky for more than 600 years.

The same Arabic root came through correctly the second time. As-sumūt, the ways or directions, plural of samt, entered Latin as azimut.[2] Astronomers and navigators use it for the horizontal angle of an object measured round from the north.

Zenith and azimuth are siblings from one root. One arrived by accident, the other intact.

The point directly below the observer, opposite the zenith, was naẓīr as-samt, the opposite of the way.[2] Latin kept the first word. English later borrowed it for the lowest point of anything, including a career or a season.

Al-kuḥl was kohl, a fine powder ground from a mineral and used as eye cosmetic. The word came to mean any substance refined to its essence, and then the essence drawn off by distillation, the process carried out with the alembic, another word from Arabic.[3]

By the time chemists named ethanol, the word had travelled from an eyelid to a laboratory.

The bottom line

Two of these words trace back to one scholar in Baghdad, and three to one Arabic word for a path. The sixth began as a cosmetic and ended as a chemical term.

The vocabulary is the easiest evidence to check, and the hardest to explain away. Science learned these words because it learned the work they described, and it learned that work in Arabic.
