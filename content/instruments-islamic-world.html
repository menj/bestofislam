The usual telling goes: Muslims preserved Greek science, Europe rediscovered it, progress resumed. It's a story that makes the middle 800 years a warehouse.

The warehouse built instruments. Some of them are still on the bench.

The Greeks had a basic astrolabe. What came out of Baghdad and Cordoba was a different object: a computer in brass, with interchangeable plates for different latitudes and a rotating star map that let you find the time, the direction of Mecca, and the position of any listed star, day or night.

Mariners carried simplified versions for centuries. The principle, projecting the sky onto a flat disc and rotating it, is the same one behind every planisphere sold in a museum shop today.

Al-anbīq. The word is Arabic, which tells you where the thing came from.

A still: heat a liquid, capture the vapour, cool it, collect what condenses. The apparatus in Jābir ibn Ḥayyān's 8th-century work is recognisably the one in a chemistry teaching lab now. Distillation is how you get pure alcohol, rose water, essential oils, and, eventually, petrol out of crude. It starts here.

A pinhole in a dark room throws an inverted image of the outside onto the far wall. People had noticed this for ages.

Ibn al-Haytham, in the 11th century, used it to settle how vision works. Light travels in straight lines from objects into the eye, not out of the eye onto objects, which is what the Greeks had mostly believed. He tested it with the dark chamber and wrote the results in a 7-volume treatise on optics that Europe read in Latin for 500 years. Every camera is a camera obscura with film in it.

Al-Zahrāwī, working in Cordoba around the year 1000, wrote a medical encyclopaedia whose surgical volume includes drawings of roughly 200 instruments. Scalpels, forceps, bone saws, a syringe, a speculum, tools for removing bladder stones and for cauterising wounds.

Many of the designs are still in use with only the materials changed. He also described using catgut for internal stitches, which dissolves on its own, and that is still the standard for sutures that don't need removing.

Less famous, more strange. A torquetum is an instrument for converting measurements between 3 different celestial coordinate systems, mechanically, without doing the trigonometry by hand.

It's attributed to Jābir ibn Aflaḥ of Seville in the 12th century. The idea, a physical device that performs a coordinate transformation, is the ancestor of every analogue computer built before electronics. A slide rule does the same kind of job for a simpler problem.

Before the telescope, precision in astronomy came from size. A bigger instrument meant finer graduations, which meant more accurate readings.

Observatories at Maragha in the 13th century and Samarkand in the 15th built instruments on an architectural scale. The Samarkand sextant was cut into a hillside, a curved track around 40 metres in radius. With it, Ulugh Beg produced a star catalogue whose positions were the best available until Tycho Brahe, and Tycho's own instruments were built on the same principle: make it enormous, and the errors shrink.

The bottom line

The alembic is on the bench. The camera obscura is in your pocket. Al-Zahrāwī's forceps are in the theatre. The astrolabe's projection is on the star chart. The torquetum's idea is in every analogue computer that came after.

Preservation would have been enough to earn a footnote. This was invention, and the instruments are still here to prove it.
