The Quran has verses on fighting, and critics quote them without their setting. Taken with the sources around them, they restrict violence more than they release it.

"Whoever kills a person other than for murder or corruption in the land, it is as if he had killed all of mankind, and whoever saves a life, it is as if he had saved all of mankind."[1] The rule is stated for the Children of Israel and affirmed for all. The Prophet called murder among the gravest sins.

"When you meet those who disbelieve in battle, strike their necks, until you have subdued them."[2] Commentators read it as the language of combat, as in any army's order. Its next words regulate captives: release or ransom. Reports in Sahih al-Bukhari say the Prophet forbade mutilating the dead and killing women and children.[3]

Abu Bakr's instructions to an army heading to Syria are recorded: do not kill a woman, a child or an old man, do not cut down fruit trees, do not destroy inhabited places, do not kill a sheep or a camel except for food, and leave the monks in their cells.[4] These are among the earliest recorded rules of conduct of this kind, and the jurists built on them. Faruqi's glossary defines jihād as "self-exertion in the cause of God, including peaceful as well as violent means", and the rules apply to the second.[5]

Islamic law bans killing non-combatants, so genocide is forbidden whoever commits it. The killing of Armenians in 1915–16 was carried out by the Ottoman government of a secular nationalist party, and many Muslim families sheltered Armenians at risk to themselves. Muslims have been victims too, as at Srebrenica in 1995.

In 2014, 126 Muslim scholars and leaders from around the world signed an open letter to the leader of ISIS refuting its use of beheading, enslavement and the declaration of other Muslims as unbelievers as contrary to Islamic law. The Amman Message of 2004 had already affirmed the legitimacy of the main schools and forbidden the declaration of Muslims as unbelievers.
