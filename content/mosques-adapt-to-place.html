A mosque needs very little: a clean place to pray, facing Mecca. Everything else is local. That is why a mosque in Mali looks nothing like one in China, and both are mosques. Five examples show the range.

When the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) reached Medina in 622 he had a courtyard built where his camel knelt. The walls were of mud brick and the roof of palm trunks and fronds. It was a place of prayer, a school, a council and a shelter for the poor.[1] Every later mosque is a copy, in some measure, of this plain courtyard. The green dome over his tomb was added many centuries later.

Begun in 785 or 786 by Abd al-Rahman I, who had escaped the Abbasid revolution and founded an Umayyad state in al-Andalus, the mosque was extended over two centuries. It is famous for its forest of double arches in red brick and white stone, which lets a very large hall be supported by short columns. After the Christian conquest in 1236 it became a church, and a cathedral was built inside it in the sixteenth century.[2]

Tradition dates the Xi'an mosque to 742, and the present buildings are mostly from the Ming period. It follows the plan of a Chinese temple: a sequence of courtyards, pavilions and gateways, with a pagoda-like minaret from which the call is given. Arabic inscriptions hang inside a Chinese building.[3] The prayer hall faces west towards Mecca, and nothing else needed to change.

In Mali, the Great Mosque of Djenne is built of sun-dried mud brick, with timber beams projecting from its towers. The current building dates from 1907, and each year the town plasters it afresh in a community festival. It is often described as the largest mud-brick building in the world. The material is local, and so is the labour that keeps it standing.[4]

The "Blue Mosque", built between 1609 and 1616 by the architect Sedefkar Mehmed Agha, answers Hagia Sophia across the square. Its cascade of domes and half-domes, six minarets and the blue Iznik tiles inside are the high point of Ottoman style. The design takes the great Byzantine dome and makes it the centre of a Muslim place of prayer.[5]
