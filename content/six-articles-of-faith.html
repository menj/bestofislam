Ask a Muslim what a Muslim must believe and you will get a short answer: six things. The Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) listed them when the angel Gabriel came to him in the form of a man and asked what imān (faith, settled conviction) is.[1]

The list is short on purpose. Each item is a door into a large subject, so here is what each one says, what it leaves out, and where it comes from.

Tawḥīd, the oneness of God, comes first, and the rest hangs on it. The Quran puts it in a single line: "Nothing is like Him."[2]

That line carries more than the number one. It says God is not a thing among things. He is not a bigger version of a king, a father or a force of nature, and nothing in creation is a fit picture of Him. The scholar Ismail al-Faruqi argued that this is why Muslim worship keeps images out and keeps the Arabic of the Quran in: nothing is allowed to stand in for the One who has no equal.[3]

It also explains the Quran's argument against rivals: "If there were more than one god in heaven and earth, both would have collapsed."[4] A second ultimate power would be a limit on the first.

Angels are created from light, according to the Prophet, and they carry out God's commands without disobeying.[5] Gabriel brings revelation, Michael is named in the Quran, and the angel of death takes souls.[6] The Quran does not give that last angel a personal name. The name Azrail comes from later tradition.

Two clarifications matter. Angels are not worshipped and have no share in divinity. And Iblis, who refused to bow to Adam, was not an angel: the Quran says he was one of the jinn.[7]

Muslims believe God sent scripture before the Quran: the Torah to Moses, the Psalms to David, the Gospel to Jesus.[8] The Quran calls itself a confirmation of what came before and a guardian over it.

This is why belief in the earlier books is a requirement and not a courtesy. A Muslim who denies that the Torah or the Gospel ever came from God has stepped outside the article of faith. The disagreement is about what has happened to the texts since, not about whether God spoke.

"There is no community to which a warner has not come."[9] That verse is the whole position. Every people received a messenger, and the Quran names twenty-five of them, among them Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus and Muhammad, while saying that others are not named.[10]

Faruqi drew a conclusion from this: since every people was sent a warner and every person is born with an innate sense of God, nobody is shut out of the knowledge that God is one.[11] The Quran calls that sense fiṭra, the natural disposition on which God made people.[12] Muslims believe in all the messengers without making distinctions among them.[13]

Life is a test and it ends in a reckoning. "Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it."[14]

The belief does two jobs. It tells a person that wrongs which no court ever sees are still seen. And it tells the wronged that the account is not closed. The Quran spends more words on the Last Day than on almost any other subject, because everything else in the creed leads to it.

The sixth article is qadar: God knows all things and His will is over all things. The Quran says, "We created everything by measure."[15]

It does not cancel responsibility. The same Quran says "Whoever wishes, let him believe, and whoever wishes, let him disbelieve."[16] Scholars explain that God's knowing what a person will choose does not force the choice, in the way that your knowing the sun will rise does not make it rise.
