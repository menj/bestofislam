Islam has a long scholarly tradition, and most of what a student reads in a Muslim school was fixed by a few people. Here are seven, in order of date, and what each one left.

Abdullah ibn Abbas was a cousin of the Prophet (peace be upon him) and a child when he died. The Companions called him tarjumān al-Qurʾān, "the interpreter of the Quran". The Prophet is reported to have prayed, "O God, give him understanding in the religion."[1]

Much of the earliest commentary on the Quran is attributed to him. Scholars of hadith sort reports under his name by their chains.

Muhammad ibn Ishaq wrote the earliest comprehensive life of the Prophet. His work survives through the edition of Ibn Hisham (died around 833) and the quotations of al-Tabari. It is the base of almost every later biography.

Scholars praised him on the campaigns, and some, such as Malik, criticised him. Later writers weigh his reports against the Quran and the hadith collections.

Sulayman Abu Dawud al-Sijistani compiled the Sunan, one of the six canonical hadith collections. It focuses on hadith used in law, and Abu Dawud noted weaknesses in the reports he included.

He is said to have chosen the roughly 4,800 hadith in the book from a far larger number he heard.

Hunayn ibn Ishaq, a Christian of Iraq, was a physician and the best-known translator of the Baghdad of his age. He translated Galen and Hippocrates from Greek into Syriac and Arabic and set a standard for accuracy.

His work is one reason Greek medicine reached Arabic and, through it, Europe. His case shows the scholarly tradition was not solely Muslim in its makers.

Yaqub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi, "the philosopher of the Arabs", worked in the House of Wisdom on mathematics, optics, music and philosophy. His essay On First Philosophy argued for the unity of God by reason.

He is the first major philosopher writing in Arabic and sits at the start of a tradition that includes al-Farabi, Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd.

Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari began as a Mu'tazili, left the school, and gave the theological position that became most common in Sunni Islam. Faruqi's study of the divine attributes explains his move: the Mu'tazila avoided anthropomorphism by treating the Quran's attributes as figures of speech, and al-Ash'ari held that they are true as stated, with the question of "how" set aside, bilā kayf.[2]

The school he began, joined by the Maturidi, is taught in most of the Muslim world today.

Abu Hamid al-Ghazali taught law in Baghdad, left his post in a crisis of doubt, and wrote Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn (Revival of the Religious Sciences), a guide to worship and character. His Incoherence of the Philosophers criticised parts of Greek philosophy as the Muslims received it.

He brought together law, theology and spirituality. His book is still read in mosques and seminaries.
