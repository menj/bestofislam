Some stories in the Quran are short on detail on purpose. The Quran leaves the names, the places and the dates out, and the commentators filled them in, with different answers. Here are five, with what the text says and what it does not.

In Surah al-Kahf, Moses meets "a servant of Ours to whom We had given mercy from Us and taught knowledge from Our presence."[1] Moses asks to follow him to learn. The man does three things that seem wrong: damages a boat, kills a boy, and rebuilds a wall without payment. Each is explained at the end by a hidden reason.

The Quran does not name him. A hadith identifies him as Khidr, "the Green One".[2] The lesson that Moses, a great prophet, could not see the reasons behind events is the point of the passage.

Dhu al-Qarnayn built a barrier of iron and molten copper against Yājūj and Mājūj, who were causing corruption, and said "This is a mercy from my Lord. When the promise of my Lord comes, He will level it."[3] They are mentioned again in Surah 21 as breaking through "from every elevation" near the end.[4]

Muslim scholars have not located the wall. Some say it is figurative, some have looked for it in the Caucasus or Central Asia, and the Quran gives no site. The warning about them is about the end of time and not a map.

Young men flee persecution, sleep in a cave and wake after 309 years.[5] The Quran says people will dispute how many they were, and tells the Prophet not to argue about it but to say: "My Lord knows best their number."[6] It gives their dog, and no names.

The puzzle is why a story with a famous parallel in other traditions is told here so sparingly. The answer commentators give is that the number and names would distract from the point.

Surah al-Baqara says that people followed what the devils taught of magic "and what was sent down to the two angels at Babylon, Harut and Marut."[7] The two said, "We are only a trial, so do not disbelieve," yet people learned from them what separates husband and wife.

It is one of the most discussed verses. Some read it as two angels sent as a test, others as two men (malikayn, "two kings") and others as a reference to a custom. Whichever reading is taken, the lesson is the same: magic is a trial and a disbelief.

The Quran names the "people of the Rass" twice, alongside 'Ad, Thamud and the people of Noah.[8] It does not say who they were. The word rass means a well or a trench.

Commentators gave many answers, among them a people of Yamama, a people who killed their prophet in a well, and the people of a place near Najd. None is certain. The Quran's purpose is to name them among those who denied their messengers and were destroyed.
