Friday noon is the weekly gathering of the Muslim community, al-jumuʿa. Anyone is welcome to attend, and many mosques host visitors. Here is what to expect so that the first visit is comfortable.

The adhān is given from the minaret or inside the mosque. It is a fixed sequence of phrases in Arabic: Allahu akbar (God is greatest), the testimony that there is no god but God and that Muhammad is His messenger, the call to prayer and to success, and the closing phrases. Ismail al-Faruqi asked English writers never to translate the words of the call, and to give them in Arabic as the Muslims say them.[1]

On Friday there is often a second call just before the sermon.

Before praying a Muslim performs wuḍūʾ, the ritual ablution: hands, mouth, nose, face, forearms, head and ears, and feet. The Quran prescribes it.[2] Most mosques have a washing area. A visitor does not need to wash unless he intends to join the prayer.

The prayer hall is carpeted and shoes are left at the door. Visitors are asked to dress modestly: for men, long trousers and a shirt with sleeves, and for women, a scarf over the hair and loose clothing. Mosques often lend a scarf.

You will see calligraphy and geometric pattern on the walls and carpet, and no pictures of people or of God. Faruqi argued that this is deliberate. Because nothing in creation can stand for God, Muslim art uses stylised pattern, repetition and the arabesque, which draw the eye on and on past the edges of the design.[3] The hall is empty by intention.

The khuṭba, a sermon in two parts with a short sit between, is given first. The imam may speak in Arabic and in the local language. The congregation is silent during it, as the Prophet said: "If you say to your companion 'Be quiet' while the imam is speaking, you have spoken idly."[4]

Then two units (rakʿa) of the prayer are performed together in rows.

Worshippers stand shoulder to shoulder in straight rows facing the Kaaba, and fill the front rows first. Women stand behind the men or in a separate room. The Prophet told the congregation to straighten the rows and close the gaps.[5]

Visitors can sit at the back, and nobody will mind. Follow the congregation if you wish. Prostrating is a part of the prayer, and you can stay seated if you prefer.

The Quran says: "When the call is made for prayer on Friday, hasten to the remembrance of God and leave off trade. When the prayer is finished, disperse in the land and seek God's bounty."[6] There is no commanded day of rest.

So Muslims go to the mosque and then return to work, and there is often food and talk afterwards.
