The Quran retells the past as a lesson. It rarely gives dates or maps, so readers ask whether anything outside the text supports it. Here are six stories, and what the other sources say. In each case, it is worth remembering that the Quran's own interest is in the lesson.

The Quran tells of a people of rock-cut homes, to whom the prophet Salih was sent, and who hamstrung the she-camel and were destroyed.[1] Outside sources do mention the people: an Assyrian inscription of Sargon II (715 BC) records a campaign against a people called the Tamudi in Arabia, and Greek and Roman writers mention the Thamud.

The place is usually taken to be Hegra (Mada'in Salih) in northwest Arabia, with its rock-cut tombs, which was the first place in Saudi Arabia on UNESCO's World Heritage list. On the way to Tabuk the Prophet told his men not to enter the place except weeping.[2]

The Quran speaks of 'Ad, a people with great pillars, destroyed by a violent wind.[3] No inscription mentions them by that name with certainty. In the 1990s a team claimed that a site at Shisr in Oman was the lost city of Ubar, which some identify with Iram, but the claim is disputed.

The sensible statement is that 'Ad is a people of the remote past of southern Arabia, remembered in the Quran and in Arabian tradition, and archaeology has not settled the matter.

The Quran tells of young men who fled persecution, slept in a cave for 309 years and woke to a changed world.[4] The story is known in Christian tradition as the Seven Sleepers of Ephesus, told in a Syriac homily by Jacob of Serugh (died 521).

The Quran does not name the city and does not fix the number, saying that some say three, some five, some seven, "and God knows best their number."[5] Muslims take the story as a sign of resurrection, with no dependence on a particular source.

The "Two-Horned One" travels east and west and builds a barrier against Gog and Magog.[6] Many classical commentators took him for Alexander the Great, and some modern scholars point to a Syriac legend of Alexander from about 629 with similar episodes.

Muslim scholars note that the Quran does not name him, and that its portrait is of a just believer who credits his power to God, which Alexander was not. Some have suggested a different figure, such as Cyrus.

Surah 105 recalls that God turned back an army with an elephant. The background is Abraha, the Aksumite viceroy of Yemen, whose inscriptions record campaigns in central Arabia in the sixth century. The Arabs dated events from "the year of the elephant", close to the Prophet's birth.[7]

Here the Quran's allusion matches a historical figure known from inscriptions, and the surah was addressed to people who remembered the event.

The Quran says that Jonah was sent to a hundred thousand or more, and that they believed and were spared, the only town in the Quran about which that is said.[8] Nineveh was the capital of the Assyrian empire, whose ruins stand by modern Mosul, and the Hebrew Bible tells the story in the Book of Jonah. A shrine of the prophet there was destroyed in 2014.
